Regression analysis

Evaluating and Interpreting a Regression Model

A regression model can produce a convincing line. But does it actually fit the data well? How certain can we be about its estimates? And what can we conclude from the results?

In The Four Assumptions of Simple Linear Regression, we explored when a fitted line can be trusted. Now, we take the next step: evaluating model fit, understanding uncertainty, and interpreting the results in a business context.

Because a model that fits the data well does not automatically provide reliable predictions or prove cause and effect.

How Well Does the Model Fit?

A regression model estimates a relationship between a predictor and an outcome. But how closely do its predictions match the observed data?

Throughout this post, we use one example: NordHome Retail fitted a simple linear regression on 24 months of data to predict monthly sales from advertising spend, both in thousands of euros (€k). The fitted line is sales = 20 + 5 × advertising spend.

R² — Coefficient of Determination

R² measures how much of the variation in observed sales the fitted model accounts for.

For NordHome, an R² of 0.85 means the model accounts for approximately 85% of the variation in monthly sales across the 24 months used to fit it. The remaining variation is not accounted for by the model.

The regression model reduces the sum of squared errors by approximately 85% compared with always predicting average sales.

R² = 1 − Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²yᵢ is observed sales, ŷᵢ is predicted sales, and ȳ is average sales. R² compares the model’s squared prediction errors with those of a baseline that always predicts average sales.

A high R² does not guarantee accurate predictions on new data or prove that advertising causes sales to increase.

MAE and MSE

MAE — Mean Absolute Error. MAE measures the average absolute difference between predicted and actual sales.

For NordHome, an MAE of €4k means the model’s predictions differ from actual monthly sales by €4,000 on average in absolute terms. Because MAE uses the original sales units, it is relatively easy to interpret.

MAE = Σ|yᵢ − ŷᵢ|nn is the number of months, yᵢ is observed sales, and ŷᵢ is predicted sales. MAE averages the absolute prediction errors.

MSE — Mean Squared Error. MSE measures the average squared prediction error. Squaring the errors gives larger mistakes disproportionately more influence.

For NordHome, an illustrative MSE of 30 (€k)² means the average squared prediction error is 30 in squared-thousand-euro units. A month with a €15k prediction error contributes 225 to the sum of squared errors, compared with just 16 for a €4k error.

MSE = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²nMSE averages the squared prediction errors, so its unit is the square of the sales unit, here (€k)².

MSE is useful when large prediction errors are particularly costly, although its squared units make it harder to interpret directly.

Adjusted R²

Adjusted R² accounts for the number of predictors in a regression model. Unlike ordinary R², it can decrease when an additional predictor does not improve the model enough to justify the added complexity.

For simple linear regression with one predictor, ordinary R² is generally sufficient. Adjusted R² becomes more useful when comparing models with different numbers of predictors.

Do not rely on R² alone

A high R² does not guarantee accurate predictions on new data, confirm that the model assumptions hold, or prove that advertising causes sales.

R² measures explained variation, while MAE and MSE measure prediction errors. AIC and BIC can help compare candidate models fitted to the same response data; lower values are generally preferred.

How Reliable Are the Estimates?

  • Coefficient. The estimated relationship. A slope of 5 means that, in our data, each additional €1k of advertising goes with about €5k more sales.
  • Standard error. The estimated uncertainty of the coefficient. The smaller it is relative to the coefficient, the more precise the estimate.
  • P-value. It measures how incompatible the data are with a true slope of zero, assuming the model and its assumptions hold. Below 0.05 is commonly called statistically significant, but this says nothing about the size or business importance of the effect.
  • Confidence interval. A 95% interval of [4.1, 5.9] is the plausible range for the true slope. If we repeated the study many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true value.

Visualizing Uncertainty

A confidence band draws this uncertainty around the whole line. It is narrowest near the average advertising spend and widest at the extremes, where there is less information.

The band is about the line, the average relationship. A prediction interval is about one future month. It is wider because individual months also fluctuate around the average.

Correlation and Causation

Understanding Correlation

Correlation measures how two variables tend to change together, either positively (both increase or decrease together) or negatively (one increases while the other decreases). The Pearson correlation coefficient quantifies this relationship on a scale from −1 to 1, as shown in Simple Linear Regression. But correlation alone does not imply that one variable causes the other to change.

Challenges of Claiming Causation

Causation means one variable directly causes changes in another. This requires rigorous conditions to prove, typically through randomized controlled experiments. Such experiments need control and treatment groups, control of all other factors, and measurable differences, which makes them complex and often impractical in real-world business settings.

Value of Correlational Research

Even without proving causation, correlation analysis can provide valuable insights and guide decision-making. For example, athletes can improve performance by observing patterns in factors like hydration and rest. You can claim a correlation, such as more water going with better stamina, but you cannot claim direct causation without proper experimental evidence.

What Can Go Wrong?

  • Patterns in the residuals. A curve or a funnel means the model is missing something, whatever R² says.
  • Influential months. One unusual month can pull the slope noticeably.
  • Extrapolation. A model fitted on €5k to €20k of spend should not be trusted at €100k.
  • Association versus cause. The model shows that spend and sales move together, not why.

Good fit does not guarantee good predictions, so validate the model on data it has not seen.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“In our historical data, every additional €1,000 in advertising was associated with about €5,000 more in sales. The estimate is reasonably precise: the plausible range is €4,100 to €5,900 per extra €1,000. The model accounts for 85% of the variation in monthly sales. That does not prove advertising caused the increase, and we should validate the model before using it for forecasts.”

Key takeaway

R² tells us how well the model fits, and the confidence interval tells us how precisely it estimates the effect. Neither alone says whether the model is useful for decisions, so report both and state the limits.
