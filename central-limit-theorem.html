Sampling

Central Limit Theorem

In Sampling Methods we saw how to pick a sample. This post answers the next question: what can a sample tell you about the whole population? The answer comes from the central limit theorem, which builds on the bell curve from Normal Distribution.

What the central limit theorem says

The central limit theorem states that the sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size increases. In other words, as your sample size grows, the distribution of sample means takes the shape of a bell curve. And as you sample more observations from a population, the sample mean gets closer to the population mean.

Imagine you want to estimate the average weight of light-duty pickup trucks. Instead of weighing millions of trucks, you collect data on a representative sample. If the sample is large enough, its mean weight will be roughly equal to the mean weight of the whole population.

Any population shape

The theorem holds for any population. You do not need to know the shape of the population distribution in advance: it can be bell-shaped, skewed or something else. Income data, which is typically skewed, is a classic example.

Example: commute time

Imagine you are studying how long working professionals in a large city take to get to work. The city has 10 million commuters, and you cannot ask all of them. Instead, you collect survey data from repeated random samples of 100 commuters. The mean commute time of the first sample is 30.8 minutes, of the second 29.6 minutes and of the third 30.4 minutes. The means differ slightly from sample to sample because of sampling variability.

Commute times are skewed: most people travel a short time, and a few travel very long. The chart shows what happens when you look at the means of many samples from such data. With a sample size of 1, the means are just the commute times themselves, and the distribution is skewed. As the sample size grows, the distribution of the means becomes more symmetric and closer to a bell curve, and it centers on the population mean.

In practice, data professionals usually take a single sample. The sample size depends on factors like budget, time, resources and the desired level of confidence in the estimate.

Conditions

To apply the central limit theorem, these conditions must be met:

  • Randomization. The sample must be the result of random selection, so every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.
  • Independence. The values in the sample must be independent: one observation does not affect another. If the sample was selected randomly, you can usually assume independence.
  • 10%. To help ensure independence, the sample should be no larger than 10% of the population when you sample without replacement, which is the usual case.
  • Sample size. The sample has to be sufficiently large.

There is no exact rule for how large that is. It depends on two things: how precise you need to be (the larger the sample, the closer the distribution is to normal and the more precise the estimate) and the shape of the population. If the population is already roughly bell-shaped, even a small sample works. Many data professionals consider a sample size of 30 sufficient in that case. If the population is extremely skewed or has many outliers, a larger sample is usually preferred, and exploratory data analysis helps you decide how large.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“We do not need to ask every commuter in the city how long the trip to work takes. We ask a random group of 100 people and calculate their average, which came out at about 30 minutes.”

“A different random group would give a slightly different average, maybe 31 or 29 minutes, but these averages cluster closely around the true value. That is why one carefully drawn sample is enough to estimate the average commute for the whole city.”

“The question for us is therefore not ‘How many people did we ask?’ but ‘Was the sample random, and was it large enough for the precision we need?’”

Key takeaway

The central limit theorem lets you infer population parameters like the mean even if you only have data on a portion of the population. The larger your sample, the more precise your estimate of the population mean is likely to be. Whether you are measuring vehicle weight or commute time, it is a useful method for better understanding your data.
