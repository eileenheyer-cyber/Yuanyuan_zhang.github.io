Inference & experiments

A/B Testing

A/B testing is a controlled experiment that compares two versions of a website, app, or marketing message, differing in one specific change, to determine which performs better. By randomly assigning users to a control group (A) or a treatment group (B), businesses can measure the impact of that change.

Instead of relying on intuition, teams use A/B testing to make evidence-based decisions about website design, marketing campaigns, and customer experiences. Performance is evaluated using metrics such as conversion rate, click-through rate, and average revenue per user. Statistical analysis helps determine whether an observed difference reflects a genuine effect or could be explained by random variation.

Why do we need A/B testing?

Statistical inference helps determine whether a difference is likely to be genuine before a decision is made.

A change in performance does not necessarily mean that a design change caused it. Statistical inference uses sample data to assess whether the observed difference is consistent with random variation or provides evidence of a real effect. A well-designed experiment, in turn, helps establish whether that effect can be attributed to the change being tested.

Imagine an online shop changes its checkout button to green. The following week, its conversion rate rises from 5.0% to 5.6%. Did the new button cause the improvement? Not necessarily. Conversion rates fluctuate naturally, and the same week might include a sale or a holiday.

A/B testing helps address two important problems:

  • Confounding variables. The conversion rate increased after the button changed, but a concurrent sale might be responsible. In an A/B test, both versions run at the same time, so external factors such as the sale are more likely to affect both groups similarly.
  • Selection bias. If customers could choose which button to see, those who selected the new version might already be more likely to buy. In an A/B test, users are randomly assigned to the control or treatment group, reducing systematic differences between them.

Randomization does not eliminate every source of bias, but it provides a stronger basis for determining whether a change actually improves performance.

How to design an A/B test

This is a common interview question. A clean answer walks through five steps: variables, hypothesis, metrics, assigning users to groups, and analysis of the results. The running example below is a messaging app that wants to test group video calling.

Step 1: Define your variables

Start by naming the cause you want to test and the effect you want to measure. This keeps the experiment focused on one relationship.

  • Independent variable. The cause, the thing you deliberately change. It is not influenced by anything else in the experiment. Here: whether a user has access to group video calling.
  • Dependent variable. The effect, the outcome you measure. Its value is expected to change because of the independent variable. Here: the number of calls per user.

A more complex test might compare several versions of the feature, or several settings of the same feature. The logic stays the same: you change the independent variable and watch what happens to the dependent variable.

Step 2: Formulate your hypothesis

A good experiment starts with a clear, testable hypothesis: a statement that connects the feature to an expected outcome. A strong hypothesis has three parts:

  • The change. What are you introducing?
  • The expected impact. What do you think will happen?
  • The metric. How will you measure it?

For the statistical test later on, the hypothesis is written as a pair of competing statements:

  • Null hypothesis (H0). The change has no effect. Group video calling does not change the number of calls per user.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Ha). The change does have an effect. Group video calling increases the number of calls per user.

The test then asks whether the data give enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis.

Step 3: Choose metrics

The goal is not simply to track activity, but to measure whether the change achieves its intended objective without negatively affecting other important outcomes.

  • Primary metric. The main measure used to evaluate the hypothesis. Choose one primary metric before the experiment begins. For example, if the hypothesis is that a redesigned checkout increases purchases, the primary metric could be the conversion rate.
  • Supporting metrics. Provide additional context and help explain the results. For example, average order value can reveal whether customers spend more per order, while session duration may offer insights into changes in user behavior.
  • Guardrail metrics. Monitor potential negative side effects. For example, a redesigned checkout might increase conversion while also increasing the payment error rate or reducing average order value.

Choose the primary metric before running the experiment. If you examine many metrics and select whichever one looks best afterward, you increase the risk of finding an apparently positive result by chance. Defining the primary metric in advance helps prevent this problem.

Before running an A/B test, ask

  1. What decision am I trying to make?
  2. What user behavior represents success?
  3. Can I measure it reliably?
  4. Is the expected effect large enough to matter?
  5. Are there guardrail metrics to ensure I'm not causing unintended harm?

Step 4: Assign users to treatment and control groups

An A/B test is a controlled experiment. The treatment is the change being tested, and users are split into two groups:

  • Control group continues with the existing experience (1:1 video calling).
  • Treatment group gets access to the new feature (group video calling).

The difference in the metric between the two groups is the measured effect of the treatment. For example, if the treatment group averages 4.4 calls per user and the control group 4.0, the feature added 0.4 calls per user. This only works if exposure to the feature is the only meaningful difference between the groups. If the treatment group happened to contain mostly power users, you could not tell whether the feature or the user mix caused the gap.

Who is eligible? Before assigning users to a group, define who should be included in the experiment. Include only users who could realistically be affected by the change. For a checkout experiment, this might mean users who reach the checkout page, rather than all visitors to the website.

Define the eligibility criteria before the experiment starts and apply them consistently to both groups. Otherwise, differences in who enters each group could distort the results and make it harder to isolate the effect of the redesigned button.

Key principle: randomization

Randomization is important because it:

  • Creates comparable groups, so the only systematic difference is the tested change.
  • Reduces selection bias by giving each participant an equal chance of being assigned to either group.
  • Balances known and unknown factors (such as age, device, or user behavior) across groups on average.
  • Supports causal conclusions, allowing you to attribute differences in outcomes to the treatment rather than other variables.
  • Makes statistical tests valid, since most inferential methods assume random assignment.

Why it matters

Without randomization, it's difficult to know whether the change caused the result or whether some other factor did. Such a factor is called a confounder, and confounders can lead to incorrect conclusions about cause and effect.

Two ways to randomize

  • Completely randomized design. Every user is assigned to control or treatment by a random process, for example a random number generated for each user ID. It is simple and works well when the sample is large.
  • Randomized block design. You first sort users into blocks of similar users, then randomize within each block. It is used to handle known nuisance factors.

A nuisance factor is something that affects the outcome but is not what you are testing. Suppose mobile users make far fewer calls than desktop users. With a small sample, pure randomization could by chance put more desktop users in one group and distort the comparison. To prevent this, block by device: split users into mobile and desktop, then randomize within each block. If a difference appears inside a block, you can be more confident that the feature caused it and not the device.

Sample size and test duration

A test needs enough users to detect a meaningful effect. The required sample size depends on four factors:

  • Significance level (α). The probability of a Type I error: concluding that a difference exists when the null hypothesis is true. It is commonly set at 5%.
  • Statistical power. The probability of detecting a specified effect when it truly exists. It is commonly set at 80%, corresponding to a 20% risk of missing that effect (a Type II error). This is a convention that balances reliability against cost: higher power requires more users, so 80% is often chosen unless missing a real effect would be especially costly.
  • Minimum detectable effect (MDE). The smallest improvement the experiment is designed to detect. Smaller effects generally require larger samples.
  • Baseline and variability. The expected starting value of the metric and how much it varies. For conversion rates, the baseline conversion rate affects the required sample size. For continuous metrics, greater variability generally requires more users.

For example, with a baseline conversion rate of 5.0%, an MDE of 0.6 percentage points (5.0% → 5.6%), a significance level of 5%, and 80% power, you need approximately 22,000 users per group. If you want to detect a larger improvement of 1 percentage point (5.0% → 6.0%), approximately 8,000 users per group may be sufficient.

Duration depends on the required sample size and the time needed to capture representative user behavior. Estimate it in advance, taking into account traffic, purchase cycles, and weekly patterns. Run the experiment until the planned sample size and minimum duration are reached. Avoid stopping early simply because the results look promising: repeatedly checking results and stopping as soon as they become significant can increase the false-positive rate unless an appropriate sequential testing method is used.

Pro tip

In interviews, clearly explain which group is the control and which is the treatment, how users are assigned, and what you are comparing. Also consider network effects, where one user's treatment can influence another user's behavior. For example, assigning interacting friends to different groups may introduce interference. In such cases, randomizing groups of related users together may be more appropriate.

Checklist for reducing bias

Before launching an A/B test, ask yourself:

  • ✅ Have I clearly defined who is eligible?
  • ✅ Are participants assigned randomly?
  • ✅ Are the control and treatment groups similar in important characteristics?
  • ✅ Are both groups exposed to the same conditions at the same time?
  • ✅ Can participants influence which group they're assigned to? (If yes, redesign the assignment.)
  • ✅ Have I verified that the allocation matches the intended split?

Step 5: Analyse results and draw conclusions

The question to ask: "Did B perform better because of our change, is the improvement large enough to matter, and did it avoid causing other problems?"

#CheckWhat to do
1Experiment validityMake sure the experiment was conducted correctly. Check randomization, and look for problems such as missing data, users who saw both versions, or unequal exposure.
2Effect sizeCalculate the key metrics and the effect, as an absolute and a relative improvement (see below).
3Statistical significanceAsk whether this difference could have happened by random chance, using the p-value.
4Confidence intervalLook at the range of plausible effects.

6SegmentsCheck important user groups carefully to see whether the new design helps some users but harms others.
7DecisionTreatment wins, no clear difference, or treatment loses.

Effect size: absolute vs relative

  • Absolute improvement. The difference between the groups.
  • Relative improvement. The percentage increase compared with the original.

Statistical significance and the p-value

If there were actually no difference between A and B, how likely is it to observe a difference this large? If that is unlikely (commonly p < 0.05), the difference is considered statistically significant.

Confidence intervals

A confidence interval shows the range of plausible effects. It tells us, for example: based on this experiment, the true improvement is plausibly between 0.40% and 0.80%.

A worked example: From data to decision

Imagine an online shop testing a redesigned checkout button. The experiment runs until both groups reach the planned sample size.

MetricControl (A)Treatment (B)
Users22,00022,000
Conversions1,1001,232
Conversion rate5.0%5.6%

The treatment group has a conversion rate 0.6 percentage points higher than the control group. But is this difference statistically significant, or could it be explained by random variation?

Step 1: Calculate the difference

The estimated effect is the treatment conversion rate minus the control conversion rate:

Difference = 5.6% − 5.0% = +0.6 percentage points

Step 2: Calculate the 95% confidence interval

For two independent conversion rates, an approximate 95% confidence interval for the difference is:

Confidence interval = (p̂B − p̂A) ± 1.96 × SE

Where the standard error (SE) is:

SE = √( p̂A(1 − p̂A)nA + p̂B(1 − p̂B)nB )

Here, p̂A and p̂B are the observed conversion rates, while nA and nB are the sample sizes.

Substituting the values gives:

  • Estimated difference: 0.006
  • Standard error: approximately 0.00215
  • Margin of error: 1.96 × 0.00215 ≈ 0.0042

Therefore, the 95% confidence interval is approximately:

0.006 ± 0.0042 = [0.0018, 0.0102]

Expressed in percentage points, the interval ranges from +0.18 to +1.02 percentage points.

The same calculation in Python:

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Data from the example
n_a, n_b = 22_000, 22_000
x_a, x_b = 1_100, 1_232            # conversions

# Conversion rates
p_a = x_a / n_a                    # 0.05
p_b = x_b / n_b                    # 0.056

# Step 1: difference
diff = p_b - p_a                   # 0.006

# Step 2: standard error and 95% confidence interval
se = np.sqrt(p_a * (1 - p_a) / n_a + p_b * (1 - p_b) / n_b)
z = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)          # 1.96
margin = z * se

low, high = diff - margin, diff + margin

print(f"Difference:      {diff:.4f}")
print(f"Standard error:  {se:.5f}")
print(f"Margin of error: {margin:.4f}")
print(f"95% CI:          [{low:.4f}, {high:.4f}]")
Difference:      0.0060
Standard error:  0.00214
Margin of error: 0.0042
95% CI:          [0.0018, 0.0102]

Step 3: Interpret the result

The entire confidence interval lies above zero, indicating that the treatment group's conversion rate is statistically significantly higher at the 5% significance level, using the corresponding two-sided test.

The interval also communicates uncertainty: although the observed improvement is 0.6 percentage points, the data are compatible with a range of positive effects.

Step 4: Make the business decision

The results support rolling out the redesigned button, provided the improvement is commercially worthwhile and no guardrail metrics show unacceptable harm.

However, statistical significance does not automatically mean business success. The team should also consider implementation costs, expected additional conversions, revenue impact, and any unintended consequences.

Key takeaways

  • Randomize. Random assignment helps make the groups comparable, allowing you to estimate the effect of a change while reducing confounding.
  • Define success before you start. Choose one primary metric, add supporting metrics for context, and use guardrail metrics to detect unintended harm.
  • Plan the experiment in advance. Determine the required sample size, statistical power, and test duration before collecting data. Avoid stopping early simply because the results look promising.
  • Check validity first. Verify the randomization, missing data, and unequal exposure before interpreting the results.
  • Look beyond the p-value. Report the absolute and relative effect sizes, and use confidence intervals to understand the uncertainty around the estimated effect.
  • Consider the whole picture. Statistical significance does not guarantee business value. Guardrail metrics, customer segments, implementation costs, and practical significance all matter when deciding whether to roll out a change.
