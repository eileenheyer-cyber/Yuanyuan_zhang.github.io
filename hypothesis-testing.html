Statistical Significance in Hypothesis Testing

A laptop maker claims that its best-selling model runs for 8.5 hours on a battery charge. After a redesign, a sample of 40 new laptops averages 8.7 hours. Is that extra 0.2 hours a real improvement, or just the luck of the draw? That is the question behind statistical significance, and hypothesis testing is how you answer it: it gives you a clear rule for deciding whether a difference in your data is real or just random noise.

Hypothesis testing builds on Confidence Intervals: both rely on the same sampling variation, but a test ends in a decision. We will first look at the building blocks, then run the full test on the laptops. After that come one- and two-tailed tests, the t-test, and power. If you want to compare two groups, A/B Testing picks up from there.

Null and alternative hypothesis

Every test starts with two competing statements about the population.

  • Null hypothesis (H0). Nothing special is going on. For example, the true average is exactly the value we already know: μ = μ0.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Ha). Something has changed: μ ≠ μ0, or μ > μ0 / μ < μ0 if you only care about one direction.

A courtroom is a helpful way to think about it. The defendant is presumed innocent (H0) until the evidence is strong enough to convict (Ha). The court never proves innocence; it either finds enough evidence or it doesn't. A test works the same way. You assume H0 is true and ask how surprising your data would be. If it is very surprising, you reject H0. If it isn't, you fail to reject it.

Significance level and p-value

Two numbers drive every test decision. The significance level α is the bar you set before you look at the data. The p-value is the evidence you calculate from the data afterwards.

α is the risk you are willing to take of rejecting H0 when it is actually true. In other words, it is your tolerance for a false alarm. With α = 0.05, you accept a 5% chance of concluding that something changed when nothing did. The most common value is 0.05, but that is a convention, not a law of nature. A smaller α, such as 0.01, makes sense when a false alarm would be costly, for example an expensive rollout. A larger α, such as 0.10, can be fine for a cheap decision that is easy to undo. The trade-off is that a smaller α means fewer false alarms, but also a smaller chance of detecting a real effect.

The p-value answers one question: if H0 were true, how likely is a result at least this extreme? A small p-value means your data would be surprising if nothing had changed.

Putting the two together gives the decision rule. If the p-value is below α, you reject H0. If it isn't, you fail to reject H0. Note the careful wording: we say “fail to reject”, never “accept”. A test works with probabilities, so it can show that the evidence against H0 is strong, but it can never prove that H0 is true.

Don't mix them up

α is your choice, made before you see the data. The p-value comes from the data. And the p-value is not the probability that H0 is true, nor does it tell you how large or important the effect is.

The rejection region

If H0 is true, the test statistic (here a z-score) follows a bell curve centered on 0. Most results land near the middle, and extreme results are rare. The significance level α decides how much of the curve counts as “extreme”. When the question is whether a value went up, all of α sits in the right tail, and the cut-off point is called the critical value.

  • Result in the middle (blue). Fail to reject H0: there isn't enough evidence.
  • Result in the red tail. Reject H0: the result is too extreme to blame on chance, so it counts as evidence for Ha.

For α = 0.05 and a right-tailed test, the critical value is 1.645. If you test for a change in either direction, α is split between both tails and the critical values become −1.96 and +1.96, the same z-scores behind a 95% confidence interval. We come back to this below.

Steps of hypothesis testing

Now let's run the full test on the laptops. The company's claim is a mean battery life of 8.5 hours with a standard deviation of 0.5 hours. Our sample of 40 redesigned laptops averages 8.7 hours. Since the standard deviation is known, a z-test fits.

  1. State the null and alternative hypothesis. The null hypothesis says the redesign changed nothing: the mean battery life of all redesigned laptops is still 8.5 hours (H0: μ = 8.5). The alternative says it went up (Ha: μ > 8.5). We only look for an increase, because that is what the engineers were aiming for.
  2. Choose a significance level. Before looking at the results, we decide how sure we want to be. We use the usual 5% (α = 0.05), which is also the company's standard for research.
  3. Find the p-value. With 40 laptops, the standard error is 0.5 / √40 ≈ 0.079, so z = (8.7 − 8.5) / 0.079 ≈ 2.53. If the redesign really changed nothing, the chance of an average this high or higher is only about 0.0057, or 0.57%. In practice you let software calculate this for you.
  4. Reject or fail to reject. The p-value of 0.57% is below our 5%, so we reject H0. There is enough evidence that the mean battery life has increased, and we call the result statistically significant.

z = (x̄ − μ0) / (σ / √n)x̄ is the sample mean, μ0 the value under H0, σ the standard deviation and n the sample size.

The critical value gives the same answer as the p-value: z = 2.53 is beyond 1.645, so it falls in the rejection region. Here the call is clear, because the p-value is far below 0.05. Still, “significant” only tells you that a 0.2-hour gain is unlikely to be luck. It doesn't tell you whether about 12 extra minutes of battery life is worth what the redesign cost.

One-tailed or two-tailed?

The alternative hypothesis decides where the rejection region sits. A two-tailed test (Ha: μ ≠ μ0) looks for a change in either direction, so α is split between both tails. A one-tailed test (Ha: μ > μ0 or μ < μ0) puts all of α in one tail, which makes the critical value smaller: 1.645 instead of 1.96 for α = 0.05.

In our example, z = 2.53 gives a one-tailed p-value of about 0.0057 and a two-tailed p-value of about 0.011. Both are below 0.05, so the conclusion doesn't change here. A one-tailed test finds an increase more easily, but it can never detect a decrease.

Choose the direction first

Use a one-tailed test only if a change in the other direction would lead to the same decision, and decide before you see the data. Switching to one tail after a two-tailed test just missed significance is a form of p-hacking. When in doubt, use two tails.

When σ is unknown: the t-test

The z-test assumes you know the population standard deviation σ. In practice you almost never do, so you estimate it with the sample standard deviation s. The test statistic then becomes a t-statistic. Because s is itself uncertain, t follows the t-distribution, which has heavier tails than the normal curve. How heavy depends on the degrees of freedom (df = n − 1 for one sample): the smaller the sample, the heavier the tails.

t = (x̄ − μ0) / (s / √n)Same shape as z, but with the sample standard deviation s instead of σ. You compare t with a critical value from the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

Here is why this matters. Imagine the engineers only had 9 prototype laptops, averaging 8.8 hours with a sample standard deviation of 0.5 hours. The standard error is 0.5 / √9 ≈ 0.167, so t = (8.8 − 8.5) / 0.167 = 1.80. But with 8 degrees of freedom the critical value is 1.860, so t = 1.80 does not reach it. We fail to reject (one-tailed p ≈ 0.055). A z-test on the same numbers would have rejected (p ≈ 0.036). A small sample carries less information, and the t-test takes that into account.

With large samples the two tests give almost the same answer, which is why z works as an approximation when n is large.

Two ways to be wrong

A test is a decision under uncertainty, so it can go wrong in two ways.

  • Type I error (false alarm). You reject H0 although it is true. Its probability is α. In our example: announcing a battery improvement that doesn't exist.
  • Type II error (missed effect). You fail to reject H0 although it is false. Its probability is β. In our example: concluding that the redesign did nothing when it actually adds battery life.

Lowering α reduces false alarms, but it makes missed effects more likely, unless you also collect more data.

Power and sample size

α directly sets the probability of a Type I error. The probability of a Type II error depends on how you design the study. The chance of detecting a real effect is called the power of a test: power = 1 − β. A common target is 80%.

Suppose the redesign really does raise the mean battery life from 8.5 to 8.7 hours, with σ = 0.5 and n = 40. That true effect is about 2.5 standard errors away from the null, so the test statistic centers on 2.53 instead of 0. Only the part beyond 1.645 leads to a rejection, which happens about 81% of the time: power ≈ 0.81 and β ≈ 0.19. So the test would catch a real gain of this size about four times out of five, and miss it about one time in five.

Four things drive power:

  • Effect size. A larger true difference is easier to detect.
  • Sample size. A larger n shrinks the standard error and pulls the two curves apart.
  • Variability. A smaller σ has the same effect as a larger sample.
  • Significance level. A higher α raises power, but also the risk of false alarms.

You can also turn this around and use it before collecting data to plan the sample size for a one-tailed z-test.

n = ((zα + zβ) × σ / δ)²δ is the smallest difference worth detecting. For α = 0.05, zα = 1.645, and for 80% power, zβ = 0.84. For a two-tailed test, use zα/2 instead of zα.

For our example, n = ((1.645 + 0.84) × 0.5 / 0.2)² ≈ 39. So 39 laptops are enough for an 80% chance of detecting a 0.2-hour gain, and our 40 were just about right. The same logic carries over to comparing two groups in A/B Testing.

Common mistakes

  • “We accept H0.” Failing to reject doesn't prove H0 is true; it only means the evidence wasn't strong enough.
  • Confusing significant with important. With enough data, even a tiny, useless difference can be statistically significant.
  • Choosing α or the tail direction after seeing the data. Decide both beforehand, otherwise the test is biased.
  • Using z when σ is estimated from a small sample. Use the t-test instead, because z makes the evidence look stronger than it is.
  • Ignoring power. A non-significant result from a small sample often means the test was too weak, not that there is no effect.
  • Reporting only the test that worked. At α = 0.05, about 1 in 20 tests on pure noise looks significant.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“Before the redesign, the average battery life was 8.5 hours. After the redesign, it increased to 8.7 hours based on a sample of 40 laptops. We then asked: could a 0.2-hour increase happen just by chance, even if the redesign had no real effect?”

“Under the assumption that the redesign had no effect, an increase this large—or larger—would occur only about 0.6% of the time. Because 0.6% is below our 5% significance threshold, we reject the idea that the redesign had no effect. The data provide statistically significant evidence that the average battery life increased.”

“But statistical significance doesn't tell us whether the 0.2-hour increase is worth the cost of the redesign. That's the business question: does about 12 additional minutes of battery life justify the investment, and do we have enough data to be confident about the size of the effect?”

Key takeaway

Hypothesis testing starts by assuming nothing has changed and asks how surprising your data would be under that assumption. Set α and the tail direction before you look, use the t-test when σ is estimated, and plan your sample size so the test has a fair chance of finding a real effect. And remember: statistical significance shows that an effect is probably real, not that it matters for the business.
