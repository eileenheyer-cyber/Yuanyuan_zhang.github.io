Hypothesis Testing

After a checkout redesign, orders seem a bit bigger. Did the new design work, or was it just a lucky week? Hypothesis testing gives you a clear rule for deciding whether a difference in your data is real or just random noise. It builds on Confidence Intervals: both rely on the same sampling variation, but a test ends in a decision. Along the way we will look at the z-test and the t-test, one- and two-tailed tests, and power. If you want to compare two groups, A/B Testing picks up from there.

Null and alternative hypothesis

Every test starts with two competing statements about the population.

  • Null hypothesis (H0). Nothing special is going on. For example, the true average is exactly the value we already know: μ = μ0.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Ha). Something has changed: μ ≠ μ0, or μ > μ0 / μ < μ0 if you only care about one direction.

A courtroom is a helpful way to think about it. The defendant is presumed innocent (H0) until the evidence is strong enough to convict (Ha). The court never proves innocence; it either finds enough evidence or it doesn't. A test works the same way. You assume H0 is true and ask how surprising your data would be. If it is very surprising, you reject H0. If it isn't, you fail to reject it.

Significance level and p-value

Two numbers drive every test decision. The significance level α is the bar you set before you look at the data. The p-value is the evidence you calculate from the data afterwards.

α is the risk you are willing to take of rejecting H0 when it is actually true. In other words, it is your tolerance for a false alarm. With α = 0.05, you accept a 5% chance of concluding that something changed when nothing did. The most common value is 0.05, but that is a convention, not a law of nature. A smaller α, such as 0.01, makes sense when a false alarm would be costly, for example an expensive rollout. A larger α, such as 0.10, can be fine for a cheap decision that is easy to undo. The trade-off is that a smaller α means fewer false alarms, but also a smaller chance of detecting a real effect.

The p-value answers one question: if H0 were true, how likely is a result at least this extreme? A small p-value means your data would be surprising if nothing had changed.

Putting the two together gives the decision rule. If the p-value is below α, you reject H0. If it isn't, you fail to reject H0.

Don't mix them up

α is your choice, made before you see the data. The p-value comes from the data. And the p-value is not the probability that H0 is true, nor does it tell you how large or important the effect is.

The rejection region

If H0 is true, the test statistic (here a z-score) follows a bell curve centered on 0. Most results land near the middle, and extreme results are rare. The significance level α decides how much of the curve counts as “too extreme”. With α = 0.05, the most extreme 5% of results fall in the rejection region. In a two-tailed test, that 5% is split between the two tails, 2.5% each, and the cut-off points are called critical values.

  • Result in the middle (blue). Fail to reject H0: there isn't enough evidence.
  • Result in a red tail. Reject H0: the result is too extreme to blame on chance, so it counts as evidence for Ha.

For α = 0.05 and a two-tailed test, the critical values are −1.96 and +1.96. These are the same z-scores behind a 95% confidence interval.

Steps of hypothesis testing

Let's go back to the checkout redesign. Before the change, orders in the shop averaged €60, and from past data we know the standard deviation is €20. After the redesign, we look at 100 orders and the average is €64. Is that €4 difference real, or could it be chance? (The numbers are made up for illustration.)

  1. Start with the null hypothesis. This is the idea that the redesign changed nothing: the average order value is still €60 (H0: μ = 60).
  2. Set the alternative hypothesis. This says the redesign did change something: the average is no longer €60 (Ha: μ ≠ 60).
  3. Decide how sure you want to be. Before you say the redesign made a difference, you fix the significance level. We use the usual 5% (α = 0.05).
  4. Calculate the p-value. This is the probability of getting results like ours if the redesign really changed nothing. With 100 orders, the standard error is 20 / √100 = 2, so z = (64 − 60) / 2 = 2.0: our average is two standard errors above €60 (the dot in Chart 1). That gives a two-tailed p-value of about 0.046.
  5. Make the decision. If this probability is very low, below your significance level, you decide the redesign probably did change something. Here 0.046 is below 0.05, so we reject H0: the data give statistical evidence that the average order value has changed. If it had been above 0.05, we would say we don't have enough evidence.

z = (x̄ − μ0) / (σ / √n)x̄ is the sample mean, μ0 the value under H0, σ the standard deviation and n the sample size.

Notice how close the call is: p = 0.046 against a threshold of 0.05. With a slightly smaller difference we would have ended up at “fail to reject”. The test gives you a clear rule for deciding, but it is not a guarantee.

One-tailed or two-tailed?

The alternative hypothesis decides where the rejection region sits. A two-tailed test (Ha: μ ≠ μ0) looks for a change in either direction, so α is split between both tails. A one-tailed test (Ha: μ > μ0 or μ < μ0) puts all of α in one tail, which makes the critical value smaller: 1.645 instead of 1.96 for α = 0.05.

In our example, z = 2.0 gives a two-tailed p-value of about 0.046 and a one-tailed p-value of about 0.023. A one-tailed test finds an increase more easily, but it can never detect a decrease.

Choose the direction first

Use a one-tailed test only if a change in the other direction would lead to the same decision, and decide before you see the data. Switching to one tail after a two-tailed test just missed significance is a form of p-hacking. When in doubt, use two tails.

When σ is unknown: the t-test

The z-test assumes you know the population standard deviation σ. In practice you almost never do, so you estimate it with the sample standard deviation s. The test statistic then becomes a t-statistic. Because s is itself uncertain, t follows the t-distribution, which has heavier tails than the normal curve. How heavy depends on the degrees of freedom (df = n − 1 for one sample): the smaller the sample, the heavier the tails.

t = (x̄ − μ0) / (s / √n)Same shape as z, but with the sample standard deviation s instead of σ. You compare t with a critical value from the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom.

Here is why this matters. Imagine a small pilot of the new checkout: 25 orders with an average of €64 and a sample standard deviation of €10, against the historical average of €60. The standard error is 10 / √25 = 2, so t = (64 − 60) / 2 = 2.0, the same value as before. But with 24 degrees of freedom the critical value is 2.064, so t = 2.0 does not reach it. We fail to reject (two-tailed p ≈ 0.057). A z-test on the same numbers would have rejected (p ≈ 0.046). A small sample carries less information, and the t-test takes that into account.

With large samples the two tests give almost the same answer, which is why z works as an approximation when n is large.

Two ways to be wrong

A test is a decision under uncertainty, so it can go wrong in two ways.

  • Type I error (false alarm). You reject H0 although it is true. Its probability is α. In our example: celebrating a redesign that did nothing.
  • Type II error (missed effect). You fail to reject H0 although it is false. Its probability is β. In our example: dropping a redesign that actually works.

Lowering α reduces false alarms, but it makes missed effects more likely, unless you also collect more data.

Power and sample size

α directly sets the probability of a Type I error. The probability of a Type II error depends on how you design the study. The chance of detecting a real effect is called the power of a test: power = 1 − β. A common target is 80%.

Suppose the redesign really does raise the average order value from €60 to €64, with σ = €20 and n = 100. That true effect is 2 standard errors away from the null, so the test statistic centers on 2.0 instead of 0. Only the part beyond 1.96 leads to a rejection, which happens about 52% of the time: power ≈ 0.52 and β ≈ 0.48. So even though the effect is real, this test would miss it almost half the time. That is why “fail to reject” never proves “no effect”.

Four things drive power:

  • Effect size. A larger true difference is easier to detect.
  • Sample size. A larger n shrinks the standard error and pulls the two curves apart.
  • Variability. A smaller σ has the same effect as a larger sample.
  • Significance level. A higher α raises power, but also the risk of false alarms.

You can also turn this around and use it before collecting data to plan the sample size for a two-tailed z-test.

n = ((zα/2 + zβ) × σ / δ)²δ is the smallest difference worth detecting. For α = 0.05, zα/2 = 1.96, and for 80% power, zβ = 0.84.

For our example, n = ((1.96 + 0.84) × 20 / 4)² ≈ 196. So the shop needs roughly 200 orders, not 100, to have an 80% chance of detecting a €4 increase. The same logic carries over to comparing two groups in A/B Testing.

Common mistakes

  • “We accept H0.” Failing to reject doesn't prove H0 is true; it only means the evidence wasn't strong enough.
  • Confusing significant with important. With enough data, even a tiny, useless difference can be statistically significant.
  • Choosing α or the tail direction after seeing the data. Decide both beforehand, otherwise the test is biased.
  • Using z when σ is estimated from a small sample. Use the t-test instead, because z makes the evidence look stronger than it is.
  • Ignoring power. A non-significant result from a small sample often means the test was too weak, not that there is no effect.
  • Reporting only the test that worked. At α = 0.05, about 1 in 20 tests on pure noise looks significant.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“Before the checkout redesign, the average order value was €60. After the redesign, it increased to €64 based on a sample of 100 orders. We then asked: could a €4 increase happen just by chance, even if the redesign had no real effect?”

“Under the assumption that the redesign had no effect, an increase this large—or larger in either direction—would occur only about 4.6% of the time. Because 4.6% is below our 5% significance threshold, we reject the idea that the redesign had no effect. The data provide statistically significant evidence that the average order value increased.”

“But statistical significance doesn't tell us whether the €4 increase is worth the cost of the redesign. That's the business question: does the additional €4 per order justify the investment, and do we have enough data to be confident about the size of the effect?”

Key takeaway

Hypothesis testing starts by assuming nothing has changed and asks how surprising your data would be under that assumption. Set α and the tail direction before you look, use the t-test when σ is estimated, and plan your sample size so the test has a fair chance of finding a real effect. And remember: statistical significance shows that an effect is probably real, not that it matters for the business.
