Hypothesis Testing

After a checkout redesign, orders seem a bit bigger. Did the new design work, or was it just a lucky week? Hypothesis testing turns that gut feeling into a clear rule for deciding whether a difference in your data is real or random noise. It builds on Confidence Intervals: both use the same sampling variation, but a test ends in a decision.

Null and alternative hypothesis

Every test starts with two competing statements about the population.

  • Null hypothesis (H0). Nothing special is going on. For example, the true average equals a known value: μ = μ0.
  • Alternative hypothesis (Ha). Something has changed: μ ≠ μ0, or μ > μ0 or μ < μ0 if you only care about one direction.

A courtroom is a good mental model. The defendant is presumed innocent (H0) until the evidence is strong enough to convict (Ha). The court never proves innocence; it either finds enough evidence or it does not. A test works the same way: assume H0 is true, then ask how surprising your data would be. Very surprising means you reject H0. Not surprising enough means you fail to reject it.

The rejection region

If H0 is true, the test statistic (here a z-score) follows a bell curve centered on 0. Most results land near the middle and extreme results are rare. Before looking at the data you choose a significance level α, usually 0.05: the risk you accept of rejecting H0 when it is actually true. For a two-tailed test, α is split between the two tails, 2.5% each, and the cut-off points are called critical values.

  • Result in the middle (blue). Fail to reject H0: not enough evidence.
  • Result in a red tail. Reject H0: the result is too extreme to blame on chance, which is evidence for Ha.

For α = 0.05 and a two-tailed test, the critical values are −1.96 and +1.96, the same z-score used for a 95% confidence interval.

A worked example

The numbers are made up for illustration. Historical orders in an online shop average €60 with a known standard deviation of €20. After the redesign, a sample of 100 orders averages €64.

  1. State the hypotheses. H0: μ = 60 (no change). Ha: μ ≠ 60 (the average changed).
  2. Choose α. α = 0.05, so the critical values are ±1.96.
  3. Compute the test statistic. The standard error is 20 / √100 = 2, so z = (64 − 60) / 2 = 2.0.
  4. Compare. z = 2.0 lies beyond +1.96, inside the right-hand rejection region (the dot in Chart 1).
  5. Conclude. Reject H0. There is statistical evidence that the average order value changed.

z = (x̄ − μ0) / (σ / √n)x̄ is the sample mean, μ0 the value under H0, σ the standard deviation and n the sample size.

Notice how close the call is: 2.0 against a cut-off of 1.96. A slightly smaller difference would have led to “fail to reject”. A test is a clear rule, not a guarantee.

The p-value

The p-value answers one question: if H0 were true, how likely is a result at least this extreme? For z = 2.0 the two-tailed p-value is about 0.046. Because 0.046 is below α = 0.05, we reject H0. This is the same decision as the critical-value method, expressed as a probability.

What a p-value is not

It is not the probability that H0 is true, and it says nothing about how large or important the effect is.

Two ways to be wrong

A test is a decision under uncertainty, so it can fail in two ways.

  • Type I error (false alarm). You reject H0 although it is true. Its probability is α. In the example: celebrating a redesign that did nothing.
  • Type II error (missed effect). You fail to reject H0 although it is false. Its probability is β. In the example: dropping a redesign that actually works.

Lowering α reduces false alarms but makes missed effects more likely, unless you also collect more data.

Common mistakes

  • “We accept H0.” Failing to reject is not proof that H0 is true, only that the evidence was not strong enough.
  • Confusing significant with important. With enough data, a tiny and useless difference can still be statistically significant.
  • Choosing α or the tail direction after seeing the data. Decide both beforehand, otherwise the test is biased.
  • Reporting only the test that worked. At α = 0.05, about 1 in 20 tests on pure noise looks significant.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“We tested whether the new checkout changed the average order value. Before the redesign, orders averaged €60. In a sample of 100 orders afterwards, they averaged €64.”

“If the redesign had changed nothing, a gap this large, up or down, would show up by chance only about 4.6% of the time. That is below our 5% threshold, so we treat the increase as real, although only just.”

“The business question is whether an extra €4 per order is worth the cost of the change, and whether we should confirm it with more data before rolling it out.”

Key takeaway

Hypothesis testing starts by assuming nothing has changed and asks how surprising your data would be under that assumption. Set α before you look, treat a result in the tails as evidence against H0, and remember that statistical significance shows an effect is probably real, not that it matters for the business.
