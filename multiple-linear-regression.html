Regression analysis

Multiple Linear Regression

An online retailer looks at two customers. Both placed 6 orders last year, yet one generated €280 in net revenue and the other €520. Simple Linear Regression can model how revenue relates to order frequency, but because both customers placed the same number of orders, it cannot explain their revenue difference using that predictor alone.

Average basket size, customer tenure, and acquisition channel may help explain the gap.

Multiple linear regression estimates the relationship between an outcome and several predictors at the same time. This post builds one for net revenue per customer. The numbers come from simulated data for an online retailer, so the patterns are realistic but not from a real company.

From One Predictor to Several

The multiple regression equation

In Simple Linear Regression, revenue is modeled using one predictor. Multiple Linear Regression extends this idea by including several predictors, each with its own coefficient.

For an online retailer, net revenue might depend on order frequency, average basket size, and customer tenure.

The model

Revenue = b0 + b1 × Orders + b2 × BasketSize + b3 × Tenure

Each coefficient represents a different part of the model:

  • b0 — Intercept: The predicted revenue when all numeric predictors are zero and categorical predictors are at their reference categories.
  • b1, b2, b3 — Coefficients: Each coefficient estimates the change in predicted revenue associated with a one-unit increase in its predictor, while the other predictors remain constant.

What does “holding other predictors constant” mean?

Suppose the coefficient for Orders is €40. Two otherwise identical customers differ by one order per year. The model predicts €40 more in annual net revenue for the customer who places the additional order, assuming their basket size, tenure, and other predictors remain unchanged.

The coefficients are estimated using Least Squares. The method finds the values that minimize the sum of squared differences between actual and predicted revenue.

The Data and Its Preparation

The outcome is net revenue per customer over the last 12 months, measured in euros (€), after returns and discounts. The model uses five potential predictors:

  • Orders per year (numeric): approximately 6 on average, ranging from 1 to 14.
  • Average basket size (numeric): approximately €62 per order.
  • Customer tenure (numeric): years since the first order, averaging around 3 years.
  • Region (categorical): North, Central, or South.
  • Acquisition channel (categorical): Organic, Paid Search, or Referral.

The figures are illustrative and based on simulated data, not a real retailer.

The data contain 1,200 customers. The model is fitted on 900 of them, and the other 300 are held back for the test at the end of this post.

Multiple Linear Regression can combine numeric and categorical predictors. Categorical variables must first be encoded numerically so the model can use them.

Turning Categories into Numbers: Dummy Variables

Our model includes two categorical predictors: Region and Acquisition Channel. As explained in One-Hot Encoding, categorical variables can be represented using binary columns.

For this regression model, we use one category as the reference group and create dummy variables for the remaining categories.

For Acquisition Channel, Organic is the reference category:

This is how categorical information becomes part of the multiple regression equation.

Assumptions of Multiple Linear Regression

Multiple Linear Regression relies on the same four assumptions as simple linear regression: linearity, independent observations, normally distributed residuals, and constant residual variance (homoscedasticity). They are explained in The Four Assumptions of Simple Linear Regression. With several predictors, one more concern matters: multicollinearity.

An additional concern: Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity occurs when two or more predictors are highly correlated with each other. Suppose the retailer includes both orders per year and items bought per year. Customers who order more frequently tend to buy more items, so these predictors are strongly correlated (correlation = 0.96 in this illustrative example).

Compare two models:

  • Model A: Includes both orders per year and items bought per year.
  • Model B: Includes orders per year only.

The estimated coefficient for orders changes only slightly, from €46 in Model B to €49 in Model A. However, its standard error increases from €0.7 to €2.5, more than threefold.

Meanwhile, the estimated coefficient for items bought is −€1.2, with a confidence interval that includes both negative and positive values.

Because the two predictors are highly correlated, the model has difficulty estimating their individual contributions precisely. The coefficient for items bought is not necessarily zero; the data simply do not provide a precise estimate of its relationship with revenue after accounting for orders.

The key lesson: Multicollinearity does not automatically make a regression model useless. Predictions within the observed data range may remain reasonably accurate, while individual coefficients become less precise and more sensitive to changes in the data or model specification.

Correlation matrix or scatterplot matrix

Helps identify pairs of highly correlated predictors. However, it may not reveal multicollinearity involving combinations of several variables.

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)

VIF measures how much the variance of a coefficient estimate is inflated because a predictor is linearly related to the other predictors.

To calculate it, regress one predictor on all the remaining predictors and obtain the resulting R²:

VIF = 11 − R²R² comes from regressing one predictor on all the others.

A high R² means the other predictors can explain much of that predictor’s variation. This makes it harder to separate their individual relationships with the outcome.

For orders per year, the other predictors explain about 91% of its variation (R² = 0.9144), so VIF = 1 / (1 − 0.9144) ≈ 11.7. This matches Chart 4: the correlation between orders and items is 0.956 (0.96 rounded), so they alone already give a VIF of about 11.7, and basket size and tenure add almost nothing. Items has the same VIF for the same reason.

In Python, statsmodels calculates it in a few lines:

import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor

X = sm.add_constant(df[["orders", "items", "basket", "tenure"]])

vifs = pd.Series(
    [variance_inflation_factor(X.values, i) for i in range(X.shape[1])],
    index=X.columns,
)
print(vifs.drop("const").round(1))

X contains the predictor columns, with categorical variables encoded as dummy variables. The constant column is needed for the calculation, but its own VIF is not reported.

To interpret the result, take the square root. √VIF shows how many times larger the standard error of that coefficient is than it would be if the predictor were uncorrelated with the others. √11.7 ≈ 3.4, which matches Model A, where the standard error of orders rose from €0.7 to €2.5.

VIFInterpretation
1No linear relationship with the other predictors
1 to 5Moderate; usually no action needed
5 to 10Often treated as a warning
Above 10Can indicate substantial multicollinearity

These thresholds are rules of thumb, not strict decision boundaries. In our model, orders and items both have a VIF of about 12, while basket size and tenure are about 1.

Possible remedies

Remove one of the highly correlated predictors if it is not essential, or combine them into a meaningful measure when this makes sense for the business problem. Then refit the model and reassess the diagnostics.

The goal is not to eliminate every correlation between predictors. It is to ensure that the model provides sufficiently reliable coefficient estimates for the questions you want to answer.

Does the Model Hold Up? Adjusted R², Residuals, and New Data

Three questions decide whether the model can be trusted:

  • How much variation does the model explain? R² is 0.85 and adjusted R² is 0.85, so about 85% of the differences in revenue between customers are explained. Adjusted R² penalizes predictors that add little, which makes it fairer when comparing models. See Evaluating and Interpreting a Regression Model.
  • Do the errors behave? The residuals show no curve and no fanning, and the Q-Q points follow the line, so the assumptions above hold.
  • Does it work on customers it has not seen? On the 300 held-out customers the typical error (RMSE) is €46, against €48 on the 900 training customers. That similarity is a sign the model is not overfitted; see Overfitting and Underfitting.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“Each additional order per year goes with about €46 more net revenue per customer, when basket size, tenure, channel and region are the same. Customers acquired through Paid Search bring about €38 less per year than organic customers who otherwise look alike, and referral customers about €17 more.”

“Together these factors explain about 85% of the differences between customers, and the model works just as well on 300 customers it had not seen. It shows association, not proof that switching channels would change revenue.”

“So the question for the next budget meeting is: which channel brings customers who order more often and stay longer, and is Paid Search still worth its cost?”

Key takeaway

Multiple linear regression estimates the relationship between an outcome and several predictors at once, and each coefficient is read with the other predictors held constant. Categories enter as dummy variables measured against a reference category, highly correlated predictors (multicollinearity) inflate the standard errors of individual coefficients, and the model should be judged on adjusted R², residuals and new data, not on R² alone.
