ANOVA: Comparing Averages Across Three or More Groups

Do email, social media and search campaigns generate different average order values? And if they do, are these differences meaningful or simply the result of random variation?

Comparing two groups is relatively straightforward with a t-test. But when we want to compare three or more groups, running multiple t-tests can increase the risk of false positives.

This is where ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) comes in. It tests whether the differences between group means are large relative to the variation within each group. Instead of testing each pair separately, ANOVA evaluates whether there is sufficient statistical evidence that not all group means are equal.

Why Not Run Several t-Tests?

Imagine we want to compare the average order value of three marketing channels: email, social media and search.

We could run three separate t-tests: email vs. social, email vs. search, and social vs. search. However, every additional test increases the overall risk of a false positive — concluding that a difference exists when it is actually due to random variation.

With three independent tests at a 5% significance level, the probability of at least one false positive would be approximately 14.3% if all null hypotheses were true. As the number of comparisons increases, this risk can grow further.

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) addresses this by testing the overall question first: is there sufficient statistical evidence that not all group means are equal?

One-Way vs. Two-Way ANOVA

One-way ANOVA

One-way ANOVA examines the relationship between one categorical independent variable and one continuous dependent variable.

For example, we could investigate whether average order value differs across email, social media and search campaigns. The hypotheses are:

  • H0: The population mean order values are equal across all three channels.

    H0: μEmail = μSocial = μSearch

  • H1: Not all population mean order values are equal.

    H1: NOT (μEmail = μSocial = μSearch)

A significant result indicates that at least one population mean differs, but it does not tell us which channels differ. Post-hoc tests can investigate individual pairs.

Two-Way ANOVA

Two-way ANOVA examines how two categorical independent variables relate to one continuous dependent variable.

For example, we could investigate whether average order value differs by marketing channel (email, social media, search) and customer type (new vs. returning).

Unlike one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA evaluates three effects:

  • Main effect of marketing channel: Do average order values differ across channels?
  • Main effect of customer type: Do new and returning customers have different average order values?
  • Interaction effect: Does the effect of marketing channel depend on customer type?

Each effect has its own null and alternative hypotheses. The null hypotheses state that there is no effect or interaction, while the alternative hypotheses state that an effect or interaction exists.

The interaction effect is particularly interesting. For example, email marketing might generate higher average order values among returning customers, while search performs better among new customers. This would suggest that the relationship between marketing channel and order value depends on customer type.

The Core Idea: Variation Between Groups vs. Variation Within Groups

ANOVA does not compare the averages directly. It compares two kinds of variation:

  • Variation between groups. How far the group averages are from the overall average. If the channels really differ, their averages spread out.
  • Variation within groups. How much individual orders differ inside the same channel. Even within one channel, some customers spend €30 and others €90. This is the background noise.

The F-statistic puts the two types of variation into one ratio:

F = Between-group mean square (MSB)Within-group mean square (MSW)

If all groups share the same population mean, the F-statistic is generally around 1. The larger F becomes, the stronger the evidence against the null hypothesis — because the variation between group means is large relative to the variation within groups.

A large F-statistic does not automatically mean the result is statistically significant. We need the F-distribution and its degrees of freedom to determine the p-value.

One-Way ANOVA, Step by Step

We compare 60 orders from three marketing channels, 20 per channel. The figures are illustrative and based on simulated data, not a real retailer.

ChannelAverage order valueStandard deviation

Social€54.95€13.15
Search€71.60€13.86

Step 1: Define the null and alternative hypotheses

  • H0 (null hypothesis): The population mean order value is equal across all three marketing channels.

    H0: μEmail = μSocial = μSearch

  • H1 (alternative hypothesis): The means of each group are not all equal. Remember, even if only one mean differs, that is sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis.

    H1: NOT (μEmail = μSocial = μSearch)

We use a significance level of α = 0.05, meaning we accept a 5% Type I error rate for this test.

Step 2: Calculate the F-statistic

The overall mean of all 60 orders is €64.22. ANOVA uses this overall mean as a reference point to measure two types of variation: between-group variation and within-group variation.

1. Between-group variation: sum of squares between groups (SSB)

For each marketing channel, we calculate the squared difference between its mean order value and the overall mean. We multiply this difference by the number of orders in that group and add the results.

SSB = Σi=1k ni(x̄i − x̄)²

Here, ni is the number of orders in group i, x̄i is the group mean, and x̄ is the overall mean.

In our example:

SSB = 20 × 1.88² + 20 × (−9.27)² + 20 × 7.38² = 2,878.6Each channel has 20 orders. The three differences are the channel means (€66.10 email, €54.95 social, €71.60 search) minus the overall mean of €64.22.

A larger SSB indicates that the group means are more spread out around the overall mean.

2. Within-group variation: sum of squares within groups (SSW)

For each channel, we calculate the squared difference between every order value and its channel’s mean. We then add all these squared differences across the three groups.

SSW = Σi=1k Σj=1ni (xij − x̄i)²

In our example:

(x1 − 66.10)² + (x2 − 66.10)² + … + (x20 − 66.10)² = 6,863.80
(x1 − 54.95)² + (x2 − 54.95)² + … + (x20 − 54.95)² = 3,284.95
(x1 − 71.60)² + (x2 − 71.60)² + … + (x20 − 71.60)² = 3,650.80
SSW = 6,863.80 + 3,284.95 + 3,650.80 = 13,799.55 ≈ 13,799.6In each line, x1 to x20 are the 20 order values of one channel, and the number after the minus sign is that channel’s mean order value.

A larger SSW indicates greater variation among individual orders within the groups.

3. Why Do We Divide by Degrees of Freedom?

The between-group sum of squares (SSB) and the within-group sum of squares (SSW) both measure variation, but they are based on different degrees of freedom. Dividing each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom gives us a mean square (MS), which represents an estimate of variance.

For a one-way ANOVA with three groups and 60 observations:

  • Between-group degrees of freedom: dfB = k − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2
  • Within-group degrees of freedom: dfW = N − k = 60 − 3 = 57

Here, k is the number of groups and N is the total number of observations.

Where does 57 come from?

We have 60 orders divided into three marketing channels, with 20 orders in each channel. Because we calculate a separate average for each channel, one degree of freedom is used per group. This leaves 19 degrees of freedom per channel, giving us 57 in total. These represent the remaining independent information used to estimate the variation within the groups.

The mean squares are calculated as follows:

Between-group mean square:

MSB = SSBdfB = 2,878.642 = 1,439.32

Within-group mean square:

MSW = SSWdfW = 13,799.657 ≈ 242.10

4. Why Does This Matter?

The mean squares allow us to compare variation between groups with variation within groups.

  • MSB (between-group mean square) measures how much the group means vary around the overall mean.
  • MSW (within-group mean square) estimates the variation among individual observations within their respective groups.

If the null hypothesis is true, both mean squares estimate the same underlying population variance on average. The F-statistic should therefore generally be around 1, although random variation can make it larger or smaller.

If the group means differ substantially, MSB tends to become larger relative to MSW, resulting in a higher F-statistic.

The F-statistic is calculated as the ratio of the two mean squares:

F = MSBMSW = 1,439.32242.10 ≈ 5.95

Our F-statistic is approximately 5.95. This means the between-group mean square is about 5.95 times the within-group mean square.

However, a large F-statistic alone does not tell us whether the result is statistically significant. We need to compare it with the F-distribution or calculate its p-value.

These results are usually summarized in an ANOVA table:

Within channels (SSW)13,799.657242.1
Total (SST)16,678.259—

Step 3: Find the p-value

If the null hypothesis is true, the F-statistic follows an F-distribution. Its shape depends on two degrees of freedom: 2 for the numerator and 57 for the denominator.

At a significance level of 5%, the critical F-value is approximately 3.16. Our observed F-statistic of 5.95 exceeds this threshold, suggesting a statistically significant result.

What does the p-value tell us?

The p-value is the probability of obtaining an F-statistic at least as large as the observed value, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

In our example, the p-value is approximately 0.0045, or 0.45%. In the chart, this is the small area under the curve to the right of the observed F-statistic.

If all three marketing channels truly had the same population mean order value, an F-statistic of 5.95 or greater would occur in approximately 4–5 out of 1,000 repeated samples under the model assumptions.

Step 4: Draw a Conclusion

The p-value of approximately 0.0045 is below our significance level of 0.05, so we reject the null hypothesis. There is statistically significant evidence that not all three marketing channels have the same population mean order value. However, we still do not know which specific channels differ.

How large is the effect?

To understand the magnitude of the difference, we calculate eta-squared (η²), which measures the proportion of total variation associated with the marketing channel.

η² = SSBSST = 2,878.616,678.2 ≈ 0.173

What is SST?

SST is the total sum of squares. It measures all the variation in the order values, ignoring which channel an order came from: we take the distance of each of the 60 orders from the overall mean of €64.22, square it and add everything up. In our example SST is 16,678.2. It consists of the between-group part (SSB, 2,878.6) and the within-group part (SSW, 13,799.6). Eta-squared is the share of SST that comes from SSB.

Eta-squared is approximately 0.17, meaning that 17.3% of the observed variation in order values is associated with differences between marketing channels. The remaining 82.7% is variation within the channels.

The p-value tells us that the differences are statistically significant, while eta-squared helps us understand their magnitude. Together, they provide a more complete picture of the results.

To identify which specific channels differ, we can perform post-hoc comparisons, such as Tukey’s HSD.

What a Significant Result Does Not Tell You

A statistically significant ANOVA result is only the beginning. It tells us that not all group means are equal, but it leaves three important questions unanswered.

  • Which groups differ? ANOVA does not identify the specific differences between email, social media and search. Post-hoc tests, such as Tukey’s HSD, help us determine which pairs of groups differ significantly.
  • Why do they differ? A difference in average order value does not establish causation. Marketing channels may reach different audiences, and other factors may influence how much customers spend. A higher average for search does not prove that search causes customers to spend more.
  • Does the difference matter? Statistical significance does not tell us whether a difference is practically meaningful. With large samples, even small differences can become statistically significant. Consider the effect size, such as eta-squared (η²), alongside the actual differences in order value.

The goal is not simply to find a significant p-value. It is to understand the size, meaning and limitations of the observed differences.

Which Groups Differ? Post-Hoc Tests

ANOVA tells us whether there is evidence that not all group means are equal, but it does not identify which groups differ. To investigate individual pairs, we can use a post-hoc test.

A common choice is Tukey’s HSD (Honestly Significant Difference). It compares all pairs of group means while controlling the family-wise error rate — the probability of making at least one Type I error across the set of comparisons.

For our three marketing channels, we compare email vs. social, search vs. email, and search vs. social.

ComparisonDifference in sample meansAdjusted p-value
Email – Social€11.150.069
Search – Email€5.500.507
Search – Social€16.650.004

At a significance level of 0.05, only the difference between search and social is statistically significant. The adjusted p-values for the other two comparisons exceed 0.05.

The confidence intervals in the chart tell the same story: an interval that crosses zero includes the possibility of no difference, while an interval entirely above or below zero indicates a statistically significant difference at the corresponding confidence level.

Why does the adjustment matter?

An unadjusted test is a regular test whose p-value is used as it is, without correcting for the number of comparisons being made. Running multiple unadjusted tests increases the risk of false positives. Tukey’s HSD accounts for the multiple comparisons, helping keep the overall family-wise error rate at the chosen significance level.

In this example, the unadjusted email-versus-social comparison has a p-value of approximately 0.037, but the Tukey-adjusted p-value is 0.069. After accounting for the multiple comparisons, we do not have sufficient evidence to conclude that these two channels differ.

Assumptions and When ANOVA Breaks

Classical one-way ANOVA relies on several assumptions. Checking them helps us decide whether its results are reliable.

  • Independent observations. Each observation should be independent of the others. For example, repeated orders from the same customer may violate this assumption because those orders can be correlated.
  • Approximately normal residuals. Within each group, the residuals — the differences between individual observations and their group mean — should be approximately normally distributed. ANOVA can tolerate moderate deviations, particularly with sufficiently large and balanced groups.
  • Similar population variances. The population variances should be approximately equal across groups. A practical first step is to compare the groups’ standard deviations and use a formal test, such as Levene’s test, when appropriate.

What if the assumptions are violated?

The appropriate alternative depends on which assumption is violated.

  • Unequal variances: Welch’s ANOVA compares group means without requiring equal population variances.
  • Strong non-normality or influential outliers: Investigate the data and consider whether a nonparametric alternative, such as the Kruskal–Wallis test, is appropriate. This test compares group distributions and is not generally a direct test of means.

The goal is not to force the data to satisfy the assumptions, but to choose a method appropriate for the data and research question.

Two-Way ANOVA: Adding a Second Factor

So far the customer type was ignored. Of our 60 orders, 30 came from new and 30 from returning customers, evenly spread over the channels (10 in each combination). Two-way ANOVA tests the channel, the customer type and their interaction at once.

Interpreting the Results

  • Both factors matter. Marketing channel (F = 8.37, p = 0.0007) and customer type (F = 16.21, p = 0.0002) have statistically significant effects on average order value.
  • The interaction is significant. With F = 5.02 and p = 0.0100, the effect of marketing channel depends on customer type.
  • There is no universal best channel. Search has the highest average order value among new customers (€68.70), while email performs best among returning customers (€80.50).

The business takeaway: Marketing strategies should account for customer segments rather than treating all customers alike.

These results show associations, not necessarily causal effects. With only 10 orders per channel–customer combination, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Coding

You do not have to calculate the F statistic by hand. The f_oneway() function from scipy.stats runs the one-way test, and tukey_hsd() runs the post-hoc comparisons. The data frame orders has one row per order, with the columns channel, customer and value.

import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

orders = pd.read_csv("orders.csv")   # columns: channel, customer, value

groups = [g["value"] for _, g in orders.groupby("channel")]
result = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
print(f"F = {result.statistic:.2f}, p = {result.pvalue:.4f}")

print(stats.tukey_hsd(*groups))
print(stats.levene(*groups))

# F = 5.95, p = 0.0045
# Levene test for equal spread: p = 0.18

The groups are sorted alphabetically, so in the Tukey output 0 = Email, 1 = Search and 2 = Social. The result matches our calculation: F = 5.95 and p = 0.0045. Only the pair 1 − 2 (Search − Social) has an adjusted p-value below 0.05.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“In our last 60 orders, the three marketing channels did not bring in orders of the same value. Search orders averaged €71.60, email orders €66.10 and social orders €54.95. The ANOVA test indicates that gaps this large are unlikely to be explained by random variation alone. The one difference we can clearly confirm is between search and social, about €17.”

“The test does not tell us why. It also shows that customer type matters: email works very well for returning customers (€80.50) but not for new ones (€51.70). The business question is whether we should use email to keep existing customers buying and search to win new ones.”

Key takeaway

ANOVA tests whether the averages of three or more groups are equal, by comparing the variation between groups with the variation within groups. A large F ratio and a small p-value mean that at least one group differs.

  • Post-hoc tests such as Tukey’s HSD show which groups differ, without inflating the false-alarm risk.
  • Two-way ANOVA adds a second factor and tests the interaction between the two.

A significant result does not show why groups differ or how much the difference matters. Check the size of the effect and the assumptions before drawing conclusions.
