Tree-based models

Gradient Boosting and XGBoost

AdaBoost corrects its mistakes by giving wrongly predicted customers a higher weight. Gradient boosting is more direct: it measures how far off each prediction is and trains the next small tree to close exactly that gap. XGBoost refines this by also asking how far a prediction can safely move, and by penalizing trees that become too complex.

The example is the same online shop: will a first-time customer order again within 60 days? Part A builds gradient boosting on eight customers and ends with Python code. Part B shows what XGBoost changes. This is the second of three articles on boosting, after AdaBoost Explained.

Part A: Gradient Boosting Step by Step

Gradient boosting reaches the same goal as AdaBoost in a different way. Instead of changing the weights of the customers, it measures each mistake directly. The mistake for one customer is called the residual: what actually happened minus what the model predicted. The next tree is trained to predict these residuals, and its answer is added to the current prediction, which corrects it. In short: start with a simple guess, calculate the residual of every customer, train a small tree on the residuals, add a small part of that tree to the guess, and repeat. We follow this on eight customers, sorted by the value of their first basket:

CustomerFirst basket (€)Ordered again
120No
225No
340No
445Yes
560Yes
670No
780Yes
890Yes

Step 1: Start with a Neutral Prediction

Four of the eight customers ordered again, so the model starts by predicting a probability of 0.5 for everyone.

Step 2: Compute the Residuals

The residual is what actually happened (1 if the customer ordered again, 0 if not) minus the predicted probability:

  • Customers 4, 5, 7, 8 (ordered again): 1 − 0.5 = +0.5.
  • Customers 1, 2, 3, 6 (did not): 0 − 0.5 = −0.5.

A positive residual says “the model was too pessimistic about this customer”, a negative one says “too optimistic”. The next tree’s job is to predict these numbers from the customer’s data.

Step 3: Fit a Small Tree to the Residuals

The best single question is “Is the first basket at most €42.50?”. It splits the customers into two groups with a different average residual: customers 1 to 3 (all −0.5) and customers 4 to 8 (+0.5, +0.5, −0.5, +0.5, +0.5, on average +0.3).

Each leaf now needs one number, the correction that the tree suggests for its customers. The simplest idea is the average residual: −0.5 on the left and +0.3 on the right. There is one catch. These averages are on the probability scale, but the model adds up its corrections as scores on a different scale, the logit scale (explained in the box below Step 4). A classification model therefore has to convert the residuals into the score scale. scikit-learn does this by dividing the sum of the residuals in a leaf by the sum of p × (1 − p) of the same customers:

leaf value = (sum of the residuals in the leaf) ÷ (sum of p × (1 − p) in the leaf)

In round 1 every customer has p = 0.5, so p × (1 − p) = 0.25 for each of them:

  • Left leaf (customers 1 to 3): −1.5 ÷ 0.75 = −2.0
  • Right leaf (customers 4 to 8): +1.5 ÷ 1.25 = +1.2

Read the leaf values as instructions: lower the score for small baskets, raise it for larger ones. This conversion is one specific choice, the one scikit-learn’s classifier makes. It is a so-called Newton-style step, and Part B shows that XGBoost reaches the same number from its own objective. Other implementations choose differently, so treat the rule above as how this recipe works in scikit-learn, not as a law of gradient boosting.

Step 4: Update the Prediction with the Learning Rate

The tree’s suggestion is not applied in full. It is multiplied by the learning rate η (eta), a number between 0 and 1 that makes every step cautious, like a small step instead of a big jump. The score before any tree is 0, which stands for a probability of 0.5:

New score = old score + η × leaf value

For this example we use η = 0.5, a large value chosen only to keep the arithmetic visible. Customers 1 to 3 get 0 + 0.5 × (−2.0) = −1.0, and customers 4 to 8 get 0 + 0.5 × 1.2 = +0.6. Converting back with p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score) gives probabilities of 0.27 and 0.65. The predicted probabilities have moved in the right direction for seven of the eight customers; only customer 6 (€70, no repeat order) moved the wrong way.

The logit scale

A probability has to stay between 0 and 1, but corrections that are added together can go beyond that. So boosted models add the corrections on a different scale, the logit scale, which has no limits, and convert the result back to a probability at the end. The logit of a probability p is ln(p ÷ (1 − p)), the same scale on which Logistic Regression works. The conversion back is p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score). A score of 0 means p = 0.50, a score of +2 means p = 0.88 and a score of −2 means p = 0.12.

Repeat: Each Tree Corrects What Is Left

Now the residuals are recomputed from the new predictions: customers 1 to 3 have −0.27, customers 4, 5, 7, 8 have +0.35, and customer 6 has −0.65. The second tree finds a new best question, “Is the first basket at most €75?”. It notices that the two largest baskets (customers 7 and 8) are still underestimated and customer 6 is still overestimated, and gives the leaves −0.58 and +1.55. A third tree repeats the cycle.

The average size of the residuals (ignoring their sign) falls from 0.50 at the start to 0.36, 0.31 and 0.25 after one, two and three trees, so it halves within three small trees. This is the number the model tries to push down at every step.

Why It Is Called Gradient Boosting

So far a residual was simply “actual minus predicted”. There is a second way to see it, and it explains the name. The model measures how bad a prediction is with a loss function: a number that is small when the prediction is good and large when it is bad. For repeat orders the usual choice is the log loss. Chart 3 shows it for one customer who did order again. The horizontal axis is the model’s score for this customer, the vertical axis is the loss.

At a score of 0 the loss is 0.69, and the curve slopes downward: raising the score lowers the loss. The slope of the loss curve is called its gradient. For the log loss, the gradient at a customer’s score is p − y, which is 0.5 − 1 = −0.5 for this customer. To make the loss smaller, you move against the gradient, in the direction of y − p = +0.5. And y − p is exactly the residual.

negative gradient of the log loss = y − p = residual

That is the secret behind the name. Fitting a tree to the residuals means fitting a tree to the negative gradients, and adding it to the model is one step downhill on the loss, taken for all customers at once. The recipe works for any loss function that has a slope. With squared error, the usual loss for predicting a number, the loss is ½ × (y − ŷ)², and its negative gradient is again the plain residual y − ŷ. For other loss functions the numbers that the trees are fitted to are called pseudo-residuals, because they are no longer simply “actual minus predicted”.

Regression and Classification

The recipe is the same for both. Only the loss function and the scale of the scores change.

  • Regression predicts a number, for example the value of a customer’s next order. The loss is the squared error, the scores are the predictions themselves, the residual is y − ŷ and a leaf usually gets the average residual of its customers. In scikit-learn this is GradientBoostingRegressor.
  • Classification predicts a category, for example a repeat order or not. The loss is the log loss, the scores live on the logit scale and are converted into probabilities, and the leaves are converted as in Step 3. In scikit-learn this is GradientBoostingClassifier. With more than two categories it builds one tree per category in every round.

Learning Rate, Number of Trees and Tree Depth

Boosting keeps correcting mistakes, and that is also its risk. Once the real patterns are learned, the mistakes that are left are mostly random noise, and a model that keeps chasing them starts to memorize the training customers instead of learning patterns that also hold for new customers. This is called overfitting. The numbers in this section come from a simulated table of 5,000 first-time customers, 36.7% of whom ordered again within 60 days. The columns are first_basket_value, used_discount, delivery_days, returned_item, email_opt_in and the target repeat_60d. 75% of the customers (3,750) are used for training and 25% (1,250) are kept as a test set. Always predicting “no repeat order” would be right for 63.4% of the test customers.

One pattern in this table matters for everything below. Among the 3,750 training customers, the share who ordered again depends on the discount code and the delivery time together:

Delivered in under 5 daysDelivered in 5 days or more
Used a discount code41.7% (997 customers)0.4% (550 customers)
No discount code52.7% (1,425 customers)26.5% (778 customers)

A late delivery lowers repeat orders for everyone, but together with a discount code it almost removes them: only 2 of 550 customers ordered again. A single question about the discount or the delivery time does not capture this. It takes both together, which is the kind of pattern that later trees in a boosted model pick up.

Learning Rate and Number of Trees

The learning rate η from Step 4 controls how much of each tree’s correction is applied, and it works together with the number of trees:

  • Large η (for example 0.5): every tree makes a big correction, so the model learns fast but also memorizes the training data quickly.
  • Small η (for example 0.1): every tree makes a small correction, so many more trees are needed, but the model approaches the pattern gently.

With all five features and a tree depth of 3, the two settings look like this:

Both settings find the same best accuracy, but the small learning rate degrades more slowly after it. That makes the number of trees much less critical, which is why a small learning rate with many trees is the usual choice. The cost is training time. Instead of guessing the number of trees, you can let the model decide with early stopping, explained below and used in the Python section.

Tree Depth and Other Safeguards

  • Tree depth. Shallow trees (often 1 to 5 splits) are the point of boosting. A deeper tree can capture an interaction such as the discount code combined with late delivery (see the table above) in one tree, but it also fits noise faster. Depth 1 means each tree uses one feature, as with the stumps of AdaBoost.
  • Subsampling. Each tree is trained on a random share of the customers (for example 80%). This makes the trees a little different from each other, like the random samples in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One, and reduces overfitting.
  • Early stopping. A part of the training data is set aside as a check. Training stops as soon as the score on this check data has not improved for a number of trees, because from then on the model is probably only fitting noise.

Gradient Boosting in Python

With the table prepared as in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One, training a gradient boosting model takes a few lines:

from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

gb = GradientBoostingClassifier(
    n_estimators=500, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, subsample=0.8,
    validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=20, random_state=42)
gb.fit(X_train, y_train)
  • n_estimators=500. The maximum number of trees. It is an upper limit, because early stopping usually ends training sooner.
  • learning_rate=0.1 and max_depth=3. Small, cautious steps with small trees.
  • subsample=0.8. Every tree sees a random 80% of the training customers.
  • validation_fraction=0.2 and n_iter_no_change=20. 20% of the training customers are set aside, and training stops when 20 trees in a row bring no improvement on them.

Early stopping ends training after 107 trees. On the 1,250 test customers the model reaches an accuracy of 79.4% (against 63.4% for always predicting “no repeat order”) and an AUC of 0.865. The AUC is a score from 0.5 (guessing) to 1 (perfect) that rates how well the model ranks the customers who will order again above those who will not; see Common Logistic Regression Metrics. When the model says “repeat order” for every customer with a probability of at least 0.5, 77% of those customers do order again (called precision), and the model finds 62% of all customers who order again (called recall).

Part B: From Gradient Boosting to XGBoost

XGBoost (extreme gradient boosting) was introduced by Chen and Guestrin in 2016 as a gradient boosting system that is fast on very large tables and gives more control over overfitting. It did not change what boosting is. It changed how each tree is chosen and what the model is penalized for. Three changes matter for understanding it:

  • Curvature, not only slope. It uses the second derivative of the loss as well as the first, which tells it how far a correction can go.
  • A penalty for complex trees. The goal that every tree tries to improve contains a price for extra leaves and for large leaf values.
  • One score for every question. The same numbers decide both which question a tree asks and which value each leaf gets.

There is a lot of engineering on top of this, such as fast split search, handling of missing values and parallel computing. We leave it out here and look only at the mathematics that changes the result. The residual recipe from Part A turns out to be a special case of it.

Gradient and Hessian: Direction and Curvature

Imagine walking downhill in fog. The slope under your feet tells you in which direction to go. It does not tell you how far you can walk before the ground turns upward again. For that you also need to know how quickly the slope changes, which is the curvature: on a strongly curved hill you must stop soon, on a gently curved one you can go far.

For every customer, XGBoost calculates two numbers at the current score. The first is the gradient g, the slope of the loss, which we already know. The second is the Hessian h, the curvature of the loss. For one customer, the Hessian is simply the second derivative, one number. For the log loss they are:

g = p − y     h = p × (1 − p)The gradient is the negative residual. The Hessian is the same p × (1 − p) that appeared in Step 3.

For our eight customers at the start (p = 0.5): g = +0.5 for the four customers who did not order again, g = −0.5 for the four who did, and h = 0.25 for everyone.

Plain gradient boosting uses only g. It knows the direction, but not how far to go, so the distance is set by the learning rate alone. XGBoost goes one step further: near the current score, the loss curve looks like a parabola, and a parabola is described by the slope and the curvature:

loss(score + step) ≈ loss(score) + g × step + ½ × h × step²Slope times step, plus half the curvature times the step squared.

A straight line, which is all the slope gives, has no lowest point. A parabola does, and its lowest point is at step = −g ÷ h. For our customer who ordered again, that is 0.5 ÷ 0.25 = +2.0, as Chart 6 shows. This way of using the second derivative to find the step is called Newton’s method.

The parabola is only an approximation: the real loss keeps falling as the score grows, because a customer who ordered again can always be predicted with more certainty. This is one reason why XGBoost still multiplies every tree by a learning rate. There is a second warning. Customers about whom the model is already confident have a small h. A leaf full of confidently wrong customers (g close to ±1, h close to 0) would get a huge value, and the penalty in the next section and the setting min_child_weight exist to prevent that.

The XGBoost Objective: Loss Plus a Penalty

When XGBoost adds a tree, it wants two things: the total loss over all customers should go down, and the tree should stay simple. It writes both into one goal, the objective:

objective = (sum of the losses of all customers) + (penalty for the trees)The penalty for one tree is Ω = γ × T + ½ × λ × (sum of the squared leaf values), where T is the number of leaves.

  • γ (gamma, gamma) is a price for every leaf. A split that adds a leaf has to improve the loss by more than γ, otherwise it is not worth it.
  • λ (lambda, reg_lambda) is a price for large leaf values. It pulls every leaf value toward 0, so that no single tree makes a large correction.

The loss itself is replaced by the parabola from the previous section, built from the g and h of every customer. Suppose the structure of a tree is fixed. For one leaf, add up the gradients and the Hessians of the customers in it, and call the sums G and H. The leaf value w that makes the objective as small as possible is the lowest point of a parabola again:

leaf weight w = −G ÷ (H + λ)G is the sum of the gradients and H the sum of the Hessians of the customers in the leaf.

Check it with our first tree. The left leaf holds customers 1 to 3, so G = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 and H = 3 × 0.25 = 0.75. With λ = 0 the leaf weight is −1.5 ÷ 0.75 = −2.0. The right leaf holds customers 4 to 8, with G = −1.5 and H = 1.25, so the weight is +1.2. These are exactly the leaf values from Step 3. With λ = 0, −G ÷ H is the same as the sum of the residuals divided by the sum of p × (1 − p). The difference is where it comes from: scikit-learn applies the rule after the tree has been found, while XGBoost derives it from its objective and uses it to find the tree. With λ = 1 the weights become −1.5 ÷ 1.75 = −0.86 and +1.5 ÷ 2.25 = +0.67: smaller, more cautious corrections.

Leaf Weights and Split Gain

How does XGBoost choose the question a tree asks? It compares the objective before and after a split. The improvement is called the gain:

group score = G² ÷ (H + λ)G and H are the sums of the gradients and the Hessians of the customers in the group.

gain = ½ × (score of the left group + score of the right group − score of all customers together) − γThe left and the right group are the customers who answer yes or no to the question.

Written out with GL and HL for the left group and GR and HR for the right group, this is gain = ½ × [ GL² ÷ (HL + λ) + GR² ÷ (HR + λ) − (GL + GR)² ÷ (HL + HR + λ) ] − γ.

The score of a group measures how much that group of customers can improve the objective. It is large when the gradients in the group all point the same way, because then they add up in G. It is small when they cancel each other: a group with as many customers who need a higher score as customers who need a lower one has G close to 0 and nothing to gain. A good split therefore separates customers whose gradients point in opposite directions. The last term subtracts the price γ for the extra leaf.

Chart 7 works this out for our first split. Without a penalty (λ = 0, γ = 0) the gain is ½ × (1.5² ÷ 0.75 + 1.5² ÷ 1.25 − 0² ÷ 2.0) = ½ × (3.0 + 1.8 − 0) = 2.4. The question about €75 would score only 1.33, and the question about €52.50 only 1.0, so €42.50 is the best first question, the same as in Part A.

The penalties change the picture. A larger λ shrinks the gain and the leaf weights, and γ can remove a split altogether:

SettingsGain minus the price per leafLeaf weights
λ = 0, γ = 02.40−2.00 and +1.20
λ = 1, γ = 01.14−0.86 and +0.67
λ = 1, γ = 1.5−0.36, no splitone leaf only

XGBoost evaluates every candidate question this way and takes the one with the highest gain. If even the best gain is not above γ, the tree stops growing there. On large tables XGBoost does not test every possible threshold but uses faster approximations; the third article of this series compares how XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost do this.

What Is Different from the Residual Recipe?

Now the two recipes can be compared directly. The left column describes scikit-learn’s gradient boosting classifier as used in Part A.

scikit-learn gradient boostingXGBoost
Choosing the splitsThe splits that best predict the residualsThe splits with the highest gain from G, H, λ and γ
Leaf valueSum of residuals ÷ sum of p × (1 − p), calculated after the splits are chosen−G ÷ (H + λ), part of the objective
Keeping trees simpleLearning rate, depth, subsampling, minimum leaf sizeThe same, plus λ, γ and min_child_weight

These are the choices of two specific libraries. “Gradient boosting” names a family of methods that all add trees fitted to the negative gradient, and implementations differ in how they pick splits and leaf values. XGBoost is the member of the family that uses second-order information throughout. On our customer table the two models end up practically the same, as the next section shows.

XGBoost in Python

Checking the hand calculation on the eight customers

First we let XGBoost build one tree on the eight customers with the settings of our calculation: a depth of 1, a learning rate of 1 so that the leaf weights are not scaled down, and λ = 0. The setting min_child_weight=0 is needed because XGBoost by default refuses leaves with a total Hessian H below 1, and our left leaf has H = 0.75.

import numpy as np
from xgboost import XGBClassifier

basket = np.array([20, 25, 40, 45, 60, 70, 80, 90]).reshape(-1, 1)
repeat = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1])

toy = XGBClassifier(n_estimators=1, max_depth=1, learning_rate=1.0,
                    reg_lambda=0, min_child_weight=0, base_score=0.5,
                    tree_method="exact")
toy.fit(basket, repeat)

tree = toy.get_booster().trees_to_dataframe()
print("split at", tree.loc[0, "Split"])
print(tree[tree["Feature"] == "Leaf"][["Node", "Gain", "Cover"]])

For the leaves, the column Gain holds the leaf weight and Cover holds the sum of the Hessians. The output shows a split at a basket of 42.5, a weight of −2.0 with H = 0.75 for the left leaf and +1.2 with H = 1.25 for the right leaf. This is exactly the hand calculation. With the default reg_lambda=1 the weights become −0.857 and +0.667.

On the full customer table

On the full table the settings are the ones from Part A plus the penalties. We set aside 20% of the training customers as a check set for early stopping.

from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from xgboost import XGBClassifier

X_fit, X_val, y_fit, y_val = train_test_split(
    X_train, y_train, test_size=0.2, stratify=y_train, random_state=42)

xgb = XGBClassifier(
    n_estimators=500, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, subsample=0.8,
    reg_lambda=1, gamma=0, min_child_weight=1,
    early_stopping_rounds=20, eval_metric="logloss", random_state=42)
xgb.fit(X_fit, y_fit, eval_set=[(X_val, y_val)], verbose=False)
print(xgb.best_iteration + 1)    # number of trees used for predictions
  • learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, subsample=0.8. The same small, cautious steps as in Part A.
  • reg_lambda=1 and gamma=0. The penalties λ and γ from the objective. These are also the defaults.
  • min_child_weight=1. A leaf must have a total Hessian of at least 1, which keeps leaves from forming around a few confident customers.
  • early_stopping_rounds=20. Training stops when 20 trees in a row bring no improvement on the check set.

Early stopping selects 95 trees. On the 1,250 test customers the model reaches an accuracy of 79.0% and an AUC of 0.865, with a precision of 77% and a recall of 62%. The scikit-learn model from Part A (107 trees) reached 79.4% and 0.865. With 1,250 test customers, one accuracy figure is uncertain by about one percentage point, so the two models are practically the same. Switching the penalties off (reg_lambda=0, min_child_weight=0) gave 79.7% and an AUC of 0.866, again within that noise, so on this table we cannot say that either setting is better.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is gradient boosting the same as XGBoost?

No. Gradient boosting is the general method of adding small trees that correct the remaining error. XGBoost is one implementation of it, with its own objective that adds a penalty for complex trees and uses second-order information. scikit-learn has another implementation with different choices.

Why does XGBoost use the second derivative?

The slope of the loss only says in which direction to move. The curvature, the second derivative, also says how far a step can go before the loss stops improving. Together they give a ready-made leaf weight, −G ÷ (H + λ), and a score for every possible split.

What is the difference between the learning rate and lambda?

The learning rate η scales down every finished tree before it is added to the model. Lambda (λ) is part of the objective: it pulls the leaf weights toward zero while the tree is being built, so it also changes which splits look worthwhile.

How many trees should I use?

Do not guess. Use a small learning rate such as 0.1, allow many trees, and let early stopping end the training when the score on a validation set stops improving.

Key takeaway

Gradient boosting adds small trees one after another, each fitted to the negative gradient of the loss, which for the usual losses is the residual, and adds a small part of every tree to the prediction. XGBoost keeps this recipe but also uses the curvature of the loss: from the sums G and H in a leaf it derives the leaf weight −G ÷ (H + λ) and a gain for every split, and it charges the penalties λ and γ for large corrections and extra leaves. On our customer table it ends up practically level with scikit-learn’s gradient boosting.
