Boosting: Learning from the Mistakes of Previous Trees

An online shop trains a decision tree to predict which first-time customers will order again within 60 days. It gets most of them right, but keeps missing one group: customers who used a discount code and received their package late. Instead of discarding the tree, the shop trains a second tree that concentrates on exactly those customers, then a third that concentrates on what the first two still get wrong.

This is boosting: many small trees built one after another, each correcting the mistakes of the ones before. Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One trains its trees independently and lets them vote. Boosting trains them in sequence and adds up their corrections.

This page is the overview of a series on boosting. It explains the idea and how boosting differs from a random forest, and it points to one article for each method: AdaBoost, gradient boosting and XGBoost, and XGBoost vs. LightGBM vs. CatBoost.

Bagging vs. Boosting: Parallel vs. Sequential Learning

A random forest and a boosted model both combine many decision trees, but in opposite ways. A forest grows deep trees (fully grown by default) independently of each other, each on a different random sample of the customers, and lets them vote. This mainly reduces variance: a single deep tree changes a lot when the training customers change, and averaging many trees steadies it. Boosting grows small trees one after another, and every new tree is trained on what the earlier trees still get wrong. This mainly reduces bias: a model that is too simple misses real patterns, and each new tree adds a pattern that was still missing. Both terms are explained in Overfitting and Underfitting.

Random forestBoosting
How trees are builtIndependently, in parallelOne after another
Size of each treeDeep, fully grown by defaultShallow, often 1 to 5 splits
What each tree focuses onA random sample of customers and featuresThe mistakes of the trees before it
Main effectLower varianceLower bias

The small trees of boosting are called weak learners, which means each one is only a little better than guessing. The most common one is a stump, a tree with just one question and two possible answers (see Decision Trees: Splitting Data into Clear Decisions). One stump is almost useless, but many stumps, each aimed at the mistakes of the ones before, add up to an accurate model. There are two main ways to aim a tree at mistakes: AdaBoost makes the customers that were wrong count more, and gradient boosting measures the remaining errors directly and trains the next tree to correct them.

Three Ways to Learn from Mistakes

Every boosting method answers the same question: how do we point the next small tree at what is still wrong? The three articles of this series answer it step by step, always with the same ten to a few thousand customers of an online shop.

A good reading order is the one in the table. AdaBoost shows the idea with the least mathematics, gradient boosting generalizes it, and the library comparison helps you choose a tool. Whichever method you use, the same safeguards apply: a small learning rate, shallow trees and early stopping keep a boosted model from chasing noise.

Boosting vs. Random Forest: When to Use Which

All models below were trained on the same 3,750 customers and tested on the same 1,250:

ModelTest accuracyAUC
Decision tree (depth 3)76.6%0.815
Random forest (300 trees, fully grown by default)74.6%0.814
Random forest (300 trees, at least 10 customers per leaf)79.0%0.866
AdaBoost (50 stumps)78.3%0.837
Gradient boosting (107 trees)79.4%0.865

On this table, a well-set random forest and gradient boosting are practically tied. What differs is the effort and the risk:

  • The default random forest fell behind, because of the noise in this table. Its trees are grown fully by default and memorize that noise. Limiting the depth to 5 to 8 levels, or requiring at least 10 customers per leaf, lifts it to about 79%. This is a property of this noisy table, not a general rule: on cleaner data a fully grown forest works well without tuning, as in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One.
  • Boosting needs more care. Learning rate, number of trees and depth work together, and a model that keeps correcting mistakes can chase random noise in the data. In return, it often reaches a slightly better score on tables with clear structure.
  • Training differs. The trees of a forest can be built in parallel, while a boosted model is built tree after tree, although modern libraries make this fast.
  • Interpretability is similar. Neither gives one readable rule set like a single tree. Both can rank the features by how much they matter. One way is to shuffle one column of the test table and see how much the accuracy drops (called permutation importance, as in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One). This ranks the first basket value first (11 points), then email opt-in (8 points) and delivery days (7 points). The discount code (5 points) and returned items (3 points) matter less.

A practical rule: start with a random forest as a strong, low-effort baseline, and move to boosting when the last points of accuracy matter and there is time to tune it.

Key takeaway

Boosting builds small trees one after another, and each tree is trained on the mistakes of the trees before it. AdaBoost does this by reweighting customers, gradient boosting by fitting the remaining errors. On our customer table it matched a well-tuned random forest, with more settings to get right.
