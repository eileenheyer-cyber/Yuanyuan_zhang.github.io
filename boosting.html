Boosting: Learning from the Mistakes of Previous Trees

An online shop trains a decision tree to predict which first-time customers will order again within 60 days. It gets most of them right, but keeps missing one group: customers who used a discount code and received their package late. Instead of discarding the tree, the shop trains a second tree that concentrates on exactly those customers, then a third that concentrates on what the first two still get wrong.

This is boosting: many small trees built one after another, each correcting the mistakes of the ones before. Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One trains its trees independently and lets them vote. Boosting trains them in sequence and adds up their corrections.

In this post, you will learn how AdaBoost reweights customers, how gradient boosting fits residuals, how learning rate and number of trees control overfitting, and what XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost add.

Bagging vs. Boosting: Parallel vs. Sequential Learning

A random forest and a boosted model both combine many decision trees, but in opposite ways. A forest typically grows deep trees, fully grown by default, independently on different bootstrap samples and lets them vote, which mainly reduces variance. Boosting grows small trees one after another and trains every new tree on what the earlier trees still get wrong, which mainly reduces bias. Both terms are explained in Overfitting and Underfitting.

Random forestBoosting
How trees are builtIndependently, in parallelOne after another
Size of each treeDeep, fully grown by defaultShallow, often 1 to 5 splits
What each tree focuses onA random sample of customers and featuresThe mistakes of the trees before it
Main effectLower varianceLower bias

The small trees of boosting are called weak learners: models that are only a little better than guessing. The most common weak learner is a stump, a tree with one question and two leaves (see Decision Trees: Splitting Data into Clear Decisions). One stump is almost useless, but many stumps, each aimed at the mistakes of the previous ones, add up to an accurate model. There are two main ways to aim a tree at mistakes: AdaBoost reweights the customers, and gradient boosting fits the remaining errors directly.

AdaBoost: Reweighting the Customers a Model Got Wrong

AdaBoost (short for adaptive boosting) keeps a weight for every customer. A customer that the current stumps get wrong receives a higher weight, so the next stump pays more attention to them. A small example makes this concrete: ten first-time customers, the question whether they ordered again, and two facts about each, the delivery time and the value of the first basket. Chart 2 below shows all ten: delivery time on the horizontal axis, first basket value on the vertical axis, and the outcome as the color.

The Starting Point: Equal Weights

Before the first stump, all ten customers count the same: each has the weight 1 ÷ 10 = 0.10, and the weights add up to 1.

Round 1: The First Stump and Its Weighted Error

The first stump looks for the single question that separates the customers best. Here it is “Was the package delivered within 5 days?”: yes means it predicts a repeat order, no means it predicts none. It is right for eight customers and wrong for customers 7 and 9, who waited 8 and 9 days and ordered again anyway.

The stump’s quality is measured by its weighted error ε, the total weight of the customers it gets wrong:

ε = 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.20

With equal weights this is simply the share of mistakes, 2 of 10. Later rounds use unequal weights, and then a mistake on a heavy customer costs more than a mistake on a light one. This number decides how much the stump may say in the final vote and how strongly the weights change.

How Much Say Does a Stump Get?

A stump with a small error should have a large say, and a stump that is no better than a coin flip should have none. AdaBoost uses:

α = 12 × ln(1 − εε) = 12 × ln(0.800.20) = 12 × ln 4 = 0.69

  • ε close to 0: the stump is almost always right, so α is large.
  • ε = 0.5: the stump is a coin flip, so α = 0 and it gets no say.
  • ε above 0.5: the stump is worse than guessing, so α is negative and its vote is flipped.

The value α = 0.69 is used twice: as the stump’s weight in the final vote, and to decide how the customer weights change next.

Reweighting: Mistakes Count More

Customers the stump got wrong are multiplied by eα, customers it got right by e−α. With α = 0.69, eα = 2 and e−α = 0.5:

  • Customers 7 and 9 (wrong): 0.10 × 2 = 0.20 each.
  • The other eight (right): 0.10 × 0.5 = 0.05 each.

These numbers add up to 2 × 0.20 + 8 × 0.05 = 0.80, not 1, so every weight is divided by 0.80. The new weights are 0.25 for customers 7 and 9 and 0.0625 for each of the other eight, and they add up to 1 again.

Two customers out of ten now carry half of the total weight. This is not a coincidence: AdaBoost always reweights so that the stump that was just used would be no better than a coin flip on the new weights. The next stump therefore cannot repeat it and has to find a new question.

Rounds 2 and 3 and the Final Vote

Round 2 repeats the recipe on the new weights. The best stump now asks “Is the first basket at most €70?”. It gets customers 7 and 9 right, but is wrong for customers 1, 3 and 10, the customers the first stump handled well. Their weights were small, so the weighted error is 3 × 0.0625 = 0.1875 and the stump gets α = 0.73. After reweighting, customers 1, 3 and 10 carry about 0.17 each, customers 7 and 9 about 0.15, and the other five about 0.04.

Round 3 gives a third stump, “Is the first basket above €52.50?”, with ε = 0.19 and α = 0.72. On its own it is right for only 5 of 10 customers, but it is right for the ones that carry weight.

The final prediction is a weighted vote. Each stump votes +1 (repeat order) or −1 (no repeat order), the vote is multiplied by the stump’s α, and the sign of the sum decides:

Score = 0.69 × h1 + 0.73 × h2 + 0.72 × h3

  • Customer 7 (8 days, €65): the stumps vote −1, +1, +1. Score = −0.69 + 0.73 + 0.72 = +0.76, so the model predicts a repeat order, which is correct.
  • Customer 3 (9 days, €45): the stumps vote −1, +1, −1. Score = −0.69 + 0.73 − 0.72 = −0.68, so the model predicts no repeat order, which is also correct.

All ten customers end up on the correct side, although the best single stump was wrong for two of them. With only ten customers this is easy; on real data AdaBoost combines hundreds of stumps.

Gradient Boosting: Fitting the Residuals

Gradient boosting reaches the same goal differently. Instead of reweighting customers, it measures each mistake directly as a residual, the difference between what happened and what the model predicted, and trains the next tree to predict that residual. The tree’s output is then added to the prediction. A second small example with eight customers, sorted by the value of their first basket:

CustomerFirst basket (€)Ordered again
120No
225No
340No
445Yes
560Yes
670No
780Yes
890Yes

Step 1: Start with a Neutral Prediction

Four of the eight customers ordered again, so the model starts by predicting a probability of 0.5 for everyone.

Step 2: Compute the Residuals

The residual is the actual outcome (1 or 0) minus the predicted probability:

  • Customers 4, 5, 7, 8 (ordered again): 1 − 0.5 = +0.5.
  • Customers 1, 2, 3, 6 (did not): 0 − 0.5 = −0.5.

A positive residual says “the model was too pessimistic about this customer”, a negative one says “too optimistic”. The next tree’s job is to predict these numbers from the customer’s data.

Step 3: Fit a Small Tree to the Residuals

The best single question is “Is the first basket at most €42.50?”. It splits the customers into two groups with a different average residual: customers 1 to 3 (all −0.5) and customers 4 to 8 (+0.5, +0.5, −0.5, +0.5, +0.5, on average +0.3).

Each leaf now gets one number, the correction the tree suggests for its customers. It is based on the average residual of the customers in the leaf: −0.5 on the left and +0.3 on the right. The model keeps its prediction as a score on the logit scale (see the box after Step 4), so the average is rescaled by dividing it by p × (1 − p), which is 0.25 here because every customer has p = 0.5:

  • Left leaf (customers 1 to 3): −0.5 ÷ 0.25 = −2.0
  • Right leaf (customers 4 to 8): +0.3 ÷ 0.25 = +1.2

Read the leaf values as instructions: lower the score for small baskets, raise it for larger ones.

Step 4: Update the Prediction with the Learning Rate

The tree’s suggestion is not applied in full. It is multiplied by the learning rate η, a number between 0 and 1 that makes every step cautious. The score before any tree is the logit of 0.5, which is 0:

New logit = old logit + η × leaf value

For this example we use η = 0.5, a large value chosen only to keep the arithmetic visible. Customers 1 to 3 get 0 + 0.5 × (−2.0) = −1.0, and customers 4 to 8 get 0 + 0.5 × 1.2 = +0.6. Converting back with p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−logit) gives probabilities of 0.27 and 0.65. The predicted probabilities have moved in the right direction for seven of the eight customers; only customer 6 (€70, no repeat order) moved the wrong way.

Leaf values and the logit scale

The logit of a probability p is log(p ÷ (1 − p)), the scale on which Logistic Regression works. Boosted trees add up on this scale because a sum of many corrections could otherwise give a probability below 0 or above 1. The final score is converted back with p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−logit). Residuals live on the probability scale, so a leaf converts them: in general, the leaf value is the sum of the residuals in the leaf divided by the sum of p × (1 − p) of the same customers. When all customers have the same p, as in round 1, this is the average residual divided by p × (1 − p), the calculation above.

Repeat: Each Tree Corrects What Is Left

Now the residuals are recomputed from the new predictions: customers 1 to 3 have −0.27, customers 4, 5, 7, 8 have +0.35, and customer 6 has −0.65. The second tree finds a new best question, “Is the first basket at most €75?”. It notices that the two largest baskets (customers 7 and 8) are still underestimated and customer 6 is still overestimated, and gives the leaves −0.58 and +1.55. A third tree repeats the cycle.

The average size of the residuals (ignoring their sign) falls from 0.50 at the start to 0.36, 0.31 and 0.25 after one, two and three trees, so it halves within three small trees. This is the number the model tries to push down at every step.

Why “gradient” boosting?

The residual, actual minus predicted probability, is exactly the direction in which the logit has to move to reduce the log loss, the quantity that logistic regression also minimizes. Fitting a tree to the residuals and adding it is therefore one step of gradient descent, taken in the space of models instead of in the space of coefficients. The same recipe works for other loss functions, which is why gradient boosting also handles regression and ranking problems.

Learning Rate, Number of Trees and Tree Depth

Boosting keeps correcting mistakes, and that is also its risk: after the real patterns are learned, the remaining mistakes are mostly noise. The numbers in this section come from a simulated table of 5,000 first-time customers, 36.7% of whom ordered again within 60 days. The columns are first_basket_value, used_discount, delivery_days, returned_item, email_opt_in and the target repeat_60d. 75% of the customers (3,750) are used for training and 25% (1,250) are kept as a test set. Always predicting “no repeat order” would be right for 63.4% of the test customers.

One pattern in this table matters for everything below. Among the 3,750 training customers, the share who ordered again depends on the discount code and the delivery time together:

Delivered in under 5 daysDelivered in 5 days or more
Used a discount code41.7% (997 customers)0.4% (550 customers)
No discount code52.7% (1,425 customers)26.5% (778 customers)

A late delivery lowers repeat orders for everyone, but together with a discount code it almost removes them: only 2 of 550 customers ordered again. A single question about the discount or the delivery time does not capture this. It takes both together, which is the kind of pattern that later trees in a boosted model pick up.

Learning Rate and Number of Trees

The learning rate η from Step 4 controls how much of each tree’s correction is applied, and it works together with the number of trees:

  • Large η (for example 0.5): every tree makes a big correction, so the model learns fast but also memorizes the training data quickly.
  • Small η (for example 0.1): every tree makes a small correction, so many more trees are needed, but the model approaches the pattern gently.

With all five features and a tree depth of 3, the two settings look like this:

Both settings find the same best accuracy, but the small learning rate degrades more slowly after it. That makes the number of trees much less critical, which is why a small learning rate with many trees is the usual choice. The cost is training time. The number of trees is then not guessed but chosen by early stopping (see the Python section).

Tree Depth and Other Safeguards

  • Tree depth. Shallow trees (often 1 to 5 splits) are the point of boosting. A deeper tree can capture an interaction such as the discount code combined with late delivery (see the table above) in one tree, but it also fits noise faster. Depth 1 means each tree uses one feature, as with the stumps of AdaBoost.
  • Subsampling. Each tree is trained on a random share of the customers (for example 80%). This makes the trees a little different from each other, like the random samples in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One, and reduces overfitting.
  • Early stopping. A part of the training data is set aside, and training stops when the score on it has not improved for a number of trees.

XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost: What Is Different?

The idea of Step 1 to Step 4 is the same in every gradient boosting library. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are engineered versions that are faster, more regularized or better with certain kinds of data:

LibraryWhat it addsGood to know
XGBoostA penalty on large leaf values and on complex trees, and a second-order step, the same p × (1 − p) idea as in Step 3Widely used and well documented; handles missing values itself
LightGBMGroups numeric values into bins (histograms) and grows each tree leaf by leaf, always splitting the leaf that helps mostVery fast on large tables; limit the number of leaves on small tables to avoid overfitting
CatBoostWorks with categorical columns directly and encodes them in an order that avoids leaking the targetOften a good result with few settings, especially with many categories

On our table of customers, XGBoost with comparable settings (100 trees, learning rate 0.1, depth 3, 80% subsample) reaches a test accuracy of 78.9% and an AUC of 0.866, practically the same as the scikit-learn model in the next section. On a table of this size the libraries differ more in speed and features than in accuracy. scikit-learn also has HistGradientBoostingClassifier, a faster, histogram-based variant in the spirit of LightGBM.

Training Gradient Boosting in Python

With the table prepared as in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One, training a gradient boosting model takes a few lines:

from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

gb = GradientBoostingClassifier(
    n_estimators=500, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, subsample=0.8,
    validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=20, random_state=42)
gb.fit(X_train, y_train)
  • n_estimators=500. The maximum number of trees. It is an upper limit, because early stopping usually ends training sooner.
  • learning_rate=0.1 and max_depth=3. Small, cautious steps with small trees.
  • subsample=0.8. Every tree sees a random 80% of the training customers.
  • validation_fraction=0.2 and n_iter_no_change=20. 20% of the training customers are set aside, and training stops when 20 trees in a row bring no improvement on them.

Early stopping ends training after 107 trees. On the 1,250 test customers the model reaches an accuracy of 79.4% (against 63.4% for always predicting “no repeat order”) and an AUC of 0.865, a measure explained in Common Logistic Regression Metrics. At the default threshold of 0.5, 77% of the customers it flags as repeat buyers do order again (precision), and it finds 62% of all customers who order again (recall).

Boosting vs. Random Forest: When to Use Which

All models below were trained on the same 3,750 customers and tested on the same 1,250:

ModelTest accuracyAUC
Decision tree (depth 3)76.6%0.815
Random forest (300 trees, fully grown by default)74.6%0.814
Random forest (300 trees, at least 10 customers per leaf)79.0%0.866
AdaBoost (50 stumps)78.3%0.837
Gradient boosting (107 trees)79.4%0.865

On this table, a well-set random forest and gradient boosting are practically tied. What differs is the effort and the risk:

  • The default random forest fell behind, because of the noise in this table. Its trees are grown fully by default and memorize that noise. Limiting the depth to 5 to 8 levels, or requiring at least 10 customers per leaf, lifts it to about 79%. This is a property of this noisy table, not a general rule: on cleaner data a fully grown forest works well without tuning, as in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One.
  • Boosting needs more care. Learning rate, number of trees and depth work together, and a model that keeps correcting mistakes can chase noisy labels. In return, it often reaches a slightly better score on tables with clear structure.
  • Training differs. The trees of a forest can be built in parallel, while a boosted model is built tree after tree, although modern libraries make this fast.
  • Interpretability is similar. Neither gives one readable rule set like a single tree. Both provide feature importance. Shuffling one column of the test table and measuring how much the accuracy drops (permutation importance, as in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One) ranks the first basket value first (11 points), then email opt-in (8 points) and delivery days (7 points). The discount code (5 points) and returned items (3 points) matter less.

A practical rule: start with a random forest as a strong, low-effort baseline, and move to boosting when the last points of accuracy matter and there is time to tune it.

Key takeaway

Boosting builds small trees one after another, and each tree is trained on the mistakes of the trees before it: AdaBoost does this by reweighting customers, gradient boosting by fitting the residuals. A small learning rate, a limited depth and early stopping keep it from chasing noise. On our customer table it matched a well-tuned random forest, with more settings to get right.
