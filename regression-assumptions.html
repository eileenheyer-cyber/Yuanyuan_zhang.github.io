The Four Assumptions of Simple Linear Regression

A regression model can find the best-fit line, but how do we know whether we can trust its results? The four assumptions of simple linear regression help us answer this question. They allow us to check whether a linear model is appropriate for our data and whether its statistical conclusions are reliable. In this article, we will explore why each assumption matters, what can go wrong when it is violated, and how to check it using visualizations and Python.

The Four Assumptions at a Glance

The four assumptions of simple linear regression help us identify potential problems before we interpret the results:

  • Linearity: Is a straight line an appropriate way to describe the relationship?
  • Independence: Can we treat the observations as independent?
  • Normality: Are the errors approximately normally distributed, as required for conventional statistical inference?
  • Homoscedasticity: Does the error variance remain approximately constant?

1. Linearity: Is a Straight Line Appropriate?

Simple linear regression assumes that the relationship between the independent variable (X, advertising spend) and the dependent variable (Y, sales) can be reasonably represented by a straight line.

As advertising spend increases, sales might also increase. But how do they increase? If sales tend to rise at a roughly constant rate, a straight line may describe the relationship well. If sales rise rapidly at first and then level off, the relationship is curved instead.

Why does it matter? If the actual relationship is curved but we fit a straight line, the model may systematically overestimate or underestimate values in different parts of the data. Its predictions and interpretation may therefore be misleading.

How can we check it? Start with a scatterplot, with X on the horizontal axis and Y on the vertical axis. Look for an approximately linear pattern rather than a clear curve. The points do not need to lie exactly on a line; what matters is that the average of Y changes in a straight-line way as X increases. Chart 1 shows the difference.

This check should happen before building the regression model. In Python, it takes a few lines:

import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="advertising_spend", y="sales")
plt.show()

When the data is very scattered, a curve can be hard to see in the raw scatterplot. A second view helps: after fitting the line, plot the residuals against the fitted values. If the relationship is linear, the residuals scatter randomly around zero. If it is curved, they form a systematic arch, because the line is too low in some regions and too high in others.

If the relationship is not sufficiently linear, one possible approach is to transform a variable, for example by taking its logarithm, and then check the relationship again.

2. Independence: Are the Observations Independent?

The independence assumption means that the error for one observation should not depend on the error for another. After accounting for the relationship described by our regression model, the remaining errors should not show a systematic connection.

Why does it matter? When errors are correlated, the model may underestimate uncertainty. Standard errors can become too small and confidence intervals too narrow, making a relationship appear more statistically convincing than it really is.

How can we check it? Start by understanding how the data were collected. For monthly observations, plotting residuals over time can reveal patterns that suggest autocorrelation.

Consider NordHome's monthly advertising spend and sales. A major campaign in March might continue to influence sales in April. If the model underestimates sales in March, it might also underestimate sales in April because both months are affected by the same underlying factor. Their errors may therefore be related.

Visualizing Independence

To investigate this, compare each month's residual with the previous month's residual. The horizontal axis shows the previous month's residual, while the vertical axis shows the current month's residual.

When errors are independent, the points should form a scattered cloud without a clear pattern. When positive residuals tend to follow positive residuals, and negative residuals tend to follow negative residuals, the points may form an upward pattern. This suggests positive autocorrelation.

The pattern is a warning sign rather than definitive proof of dependence. A formal test, such as the Durbin–Watson test, can help assess autocorrelation in regression residuals.

What is autocorrelation?

Autocorrelation means that a series is correlated with its own past values. Here, a month's residual is related to the residual of the month before. If the model underestimates March, it tends to underestimate April too. Positive autocorrelation, where high follows high, is the common case. It matters because the months partly repeat each other, so the model has less real information than it thinks.

3. Normality: Are the Errors Normally Distributed?

The normality assumption concerns the errors in the regression model. To understand it, we first need to distinguish between errors and residuals.

  • Errors are the differences between the true underlying relationship and the observed values that the model aims to describe. They represent the model's natural, unobserved noise.
  • Residuals are the differences between the observed values and the values predicted by the fitted regression model, like the −3, +2, +4, −2 and −1 from the previous post.

We cannot directly observe the true errors, so we use residuals to assess whether the normality assumption is reasonable.

Why does it matter? Normality is particularly important when we want to make statistical inferences, such as constructing confidence intervals or conducting hypothesis tests under the usual regression assumptions. A regression model can still calculate a line when residuals are not normally distributed. However, the usual inferential procedures may not be reliable, particularly in smaller samples.

How can we check it? Two common visual tools are a histogram and a quantile-quantile plot (Q-Q plot).

  • A histogram shows the distribution of the residuals. We look for a roughly bell-shaped pattern centered around zero.
  • A Q-Q plot compares the observed residual quantiles with the quantiles expected under a normal distribution. If the residuals are approximately normally distributed, the points should follow a roughly straight line.

The Q-Q plot is often particularly useful because deviations from a straight line can reveal departures from normality. With only five months, neither plot tells us much, so in practice you would run these checks on a longer history. In Python, model.resid retrieves the residuals from a fitted regression model:

import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

model = smf.ols("sales ~ advertising_spend", data=df).fit()
residuals = model.resid

sm.qqplot(residuals, line="s")
plt.show()

If the residuals deviate substantially from the expected pattern, investigate the distribution and consider whether a transformation, such as taking the logarithm of a right-skewed outcome variable, is appropriate. Then refit the model and check again.

4. Homoscedasticity: Is the Error Variance Constant?

Homoscedasticity sounds complicated, but the underlying idea is straightforward. It means that the variance of the errors remains approximately constant across the range of the model's predictions.

For NordHome, imagine that in low-spend months the predictions miss by only a couple of thousand euros, while in high-spend months they miss by ten thousand or more. If the spread of the errors systematically increases or decreases, the data may exhibit heteroscedasticity, which violates the homoscedasticity assumption.

Why does it matter? When the variance of the errors is not constant, the usual standard errors and statistical inferences may be unreliable if the issue is ignored. The regression line can still be fitted, but we need to be careful when interpreting confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

How can we check it? After fitting the regression model, create a residuals-versus-fitted-values plot. The horizontal axis shows the predicted values, the vertical axis shows the residuals, and the horizontal reference line represents a residual of zero.

Ideally, the points form a relatively random cloud around zero, with a similar vertical spread across the range of fitted values. A funnel-shaped pattern, where the spread becomes wider or narrower, may indicate heteroscedasticity (Chart 2).

The plot takes a few lines, using the model fitted above:

import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fitted_values = model.fittedvalues
residuals = model.resid

sns.scatterplot(x=fitted_values, y=residuals)
plt.axhline(0)
plt.xlabel("Fitted Values")
plt.ylabel("Residuals")
plt.show()

If the assumption is violated, possible approaches include transforming the outcome variable or using statistical methods that account for non-constant variance.

What if an assumption is violated?

A violation does not automatically mean that the entire analysis is useless. It means that we need to understand the problem before deciding how to proceed. Depending on the situation, possible next steps include:

  • Non-linearity. Transform a variable or consider a different model form.
  • Non-normal residuals. Investigate unusual observations or skewness and consider an appropriate transformation.
  • Dependent observations. Review the sampling process or use a model that accounts for dependence.
  • Non-constant variance. Consider transforming the outcome or using methods that account for heteroscedasticity.

There is no single solution that works for every dataset. Transformations also change how we interpret the variables and coefficients, so we should always reassess the model after making changes. If the assumptions remain seriously violated, a different modeling approach may be more appropriate.

Key takeaway

Fitting a line is only the first step: the four assumptions (linearity, independent observations, normal errors and constant variance) tell us whether the model's results can be interpreted with confidence. Check linearity and independence before fitting, and use residual plots for the other two afterward. The goal is not to make the data perfect, but to understand the limitations of the model and decide how to use it.
