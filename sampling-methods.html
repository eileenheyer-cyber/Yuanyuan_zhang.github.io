Sampling

Sampling Methods

A population is every member of the group you want to understand. A sample is the part of it you actually collect data from. A sample is only useful if it represents the population, and how you pick it decides that. Sampling methods fall into two families: probability sampling, which uses random selection, and non-probability sampling, which does not.

The sampling process

Sampling is a sequence of decisions. Each step builds on the one before it.

  • Identify the target population. Define the complete set of elements you want to study, such as customers who purchased a specific product.
  • Select the sampling frame. Create a specific list of individuals or items from the target population to draw your sample from.
  • Choose the sampling method. Decide between probability sampling (random selection) for representativeness and non-probability sampling (convenience or preference-based).
  • Determine the sample size. Balance precision and resource constraints to decide how many individuals or items to include.
  • Collect the sample data. Gather data from the selected sample, for example by conducting surveys.
  • Use the data to make informed decisions. Analyze the sample data to infer insights about the population and guide business or research decisions.

Carefully executing each step helps ensure that your sample reflects the population, which makes your conclusions more reliable. The next sections look at step 3 in detail.

Probability sampling

In probability sampling, selection is random. Every member of the population has a known chance of being picked, which keeps bias low and makes the sample more likely to represent the population. There are four common methods.

Simple random sampling

Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected, often using a random number generator. Example: drawing 200 customers at random from a full customer list.

Stratified random sampling

The population is divided into groups called strata (for example by age or gender), and a random sample is taken from each group. Example: sampling customers separately in each country, so that small countries are not missed.

Cluster random sampling

The population is divided into clusters, some clusters are picked at random, and all members of the picked clusters are included. Example: choosing 5 of 40 stores at random and surveying every customer in those stores.

Systematic random sampling

Members are put in order, a random starting point is chosen, and then every n-th member is selected. Example: starting at a random invoice and taking every 10th one.

Simple randomAvoids bias, accurate resultsCostly and slow for large samples

Non-probability sampling

Non-probability sampling does not use random selection, so not every member of the population has an equal chance of being picked. That means a high risk of sampling bias. The methods are cheaper and more convenient, which makes them useful when budget or time is limited, or for exploratory studies where the goal is a first understanding of a population rather than conclusions about all of it.

  • Convenience sampling. Easily accessible members are selected, such as shoppers who happen to be in your store. It often causes undercoverage bias, because less accessible groups are missed.
  • Voluntary response sampling. People choose to take part, such as an online poll. It often causes nonresponse bias, because people with strong opinions are more likely to respond.
  • Snowball sampling. A few participants recruit others. It helps reach hard-to-reach populations, but the sample can end up with similar characteristics, which biases it.
  • Purposive sampling. Participants are selected for specific needs of the study, such as interviewing only power users. It focuses on one group but excludes others, so the results are hard to generalize.

Watch for bias

Non-probability samples are practical for quick, low-cost data collection and exploration, but they carry a significant risk of bias. Data professionals need to stay aware of bias and fairness from data collection through to presenting conclusions.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“The survey shows that 90% of our customers are satisfied, but only customers who clicked the survey link answered. People with strong opinions answer more often, so this tells us how the people who responded feel, not how all customers feel.”

“If we want a number we can rely on, we should pick customers at random from our customer list and follow up until they reply.”

“The question for us is therefore not ‘How many people answered?’ but ‘Who is missing from the answers?’”

Key takeaway

How you select a sample matters as much as how large it is. Probability sampling (simple random, stratified, cluster and systematic) uses random selection and keeps bias low. Non-probability sampling (convenience, voluntary response, snowball and purposive) is faster and cheaper, but it can leave parts of the population out. Always ask who is missing from your data.
