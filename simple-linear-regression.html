Simple Linear Regression: Finding the Relationship in Data

Start with the simplest question: can we use one variable to predict another? Simple linear regression answers it with a straight line. This post explains what the model predicts and what its coefficients mean, using an advertising example from NordHome Retail. The next post shows how the best line is found, and Generalized Linear Models extends the idea to other types of outcomes.

Start with a business question

NordHome's marketing manager has next month's budget in front of her. She asks: “If we spend €6k on advertising, how much will we sell?”

She has five past months of data:

Advertising spend (€k)Sales (€k)
230
438
545
752
860

Plot these points and you see a pattern: more advertising goes with more sales, and the points sit roughly along a straight line. Linear regression finds the one straight line that fits them best, and then lets us use it to answer questions like hers.

What is linear regression?

Linear regression is a model that describes how one variable changes with another using a straight line. It has two jobs:

  • Predict. Estimate sales for a spend we have not tried.
  • Explain. Quantify how much sales tend to change when spend changes.

Independent vs. dependent variables

  • Independent variable (x). Also called the predictor: the thing we use to predict. Here, advertising spend.
  • Dependent variable (y). Also called the outcome: the thing we want to predict. Here, sales.

In this example, advertising spend is the predictor (x), and sales are the outcome (y). We use advertising spend to estimate sales, but that does not automatically mean advertising causes the observed change in sales.

The regression equation

ŷ = b₀ + b₁x ŷ is the predicted value, b₀ is the intercept and b₁ is the slope.

  • ŷ (“y-hat”). The predicted value, not the observed one.
  • b₀. The intercept.
  • b₁. The slope.

For NordHome's data, the best-fitting line is:

ŷ = 19.684 + 4.868x x is advertising spend and ŷ is predicted sales, both in €k.

Intercept, slope, and fitted line

Slope (b₁ = 4.87). Each additional €1k of advertising goes with about €4.87k more sales, on average. This is the number the manager cares about most.

Intercept (b₀ = 19.68). The model's predicted sales when advertising spend is zero. Since our observations start at €2k, this is an extrapolation. The intercept is mathematically necessary, but we cannot conclude from these data that NordHome would generate €19.68k in baseline sales.

Fitted line. The line that minimises the total squared vertical distance between the points and the line. The distance for each point is its residual: actual − predicted. For the month with €7k spend, the line predicts about 53.8 and the actual value was 52:

52 − 53.8 = −1.8 The residual for the €7k month: the actual value is 1.8 below the line.

But how does the model decide which line fits best? That is where the method of least squares comes in, and we will explore it in the next article.

Answering the manager's question

For advertising spend of €6k:

ŷ = 19.684 + 4.868 × 6 ≈ 48.9 The expected sales are about €48.9k.

b₁ = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)² b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄ Where the numbers come from: x̄ = 5.2 and ȳ = 45 are the averages, so b₁ = 111 / 22.8 ≈ 4.868. In practice, software computes this for you.

What the model does and doesn't tell you

  • Association, not proof of cause. The model describes how the two variables move together. A seasonal peak could drive both advertising and sales.
  • Stay inside the data. Predictions are only trustworthy within the range of the data (€2k to €8k). Predicting for €50k is extrapolation.
  • An average, not a promise. Individual months will sit above or below the line.

Key takeaway

Linear regression fits a line ŷ = b₀ + b₁x to data. The slope tells you how much y changes per unit of x, the intercept is the model's prediction when x = 0, and the line lets you predict, as long as you stay within the data and do not confuse association with causation.
