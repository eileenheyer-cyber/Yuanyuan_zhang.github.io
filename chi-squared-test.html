Inference & experiments

Chi-Squared Test

Do new customers pick different delivery options than returning customers, or does it only look that way in this week’s orders? An average cannot answer this, because a delivery option is a category, not a number.

The chi-squared test (χ², pronounced “kai-squared”) compares the counts we observed with the counts we would expect if the two categories were unrelated. It follows the same logic as Hypothesis Testing. A t-test or z-test compares means or proportions (see T-Test vs. Z-Test), while a chi-squared test examines whether two categorical variables are associated.

Two types of chi-squared test

The chi-squared test comes in two main forms. Both compare observed counts with the values we would expect under a null hypothesis.

  • Goodness-of-fit test. Checks whether one categorical variable with more than two levels follows an expected distribution. H0: it follows that distribution. H1: it does not. Example below: support tickets across the weekdays.
  • Test of independence. Checks whether two categorical variables are associated. It is valid when the data come from a random sample and you want to draw conclusions about a larger population. H0: the two variables are independent. H1: they are not. Example below: delivery option by customer type.

Chi-Squared Goodness-of-Fit Test: A question about one category

A support team received 120 tickets last week. The team lead assumes that tickets arrive evenly across the five working days. Counting the tickets per day gives the observed counts:

Monday stands out: 36 tickets, which is 30% of the week, while an even spread would give each day 20%. But is the uneven spread real, or just random variation?

The chi-squared goodness-of-fit test compares the observed counts of one categorical variable (here, the weekday) with an expected distribution.

Step 1: Identify the null and alternative hypotheses

We begin with two hypotheses:

  • H0 (null hypothesis): Tickets are spread evenly across the five days.
  • H1 (alternative hypothesis): Tickets are not spread evenly across the five days.

Step 2: Calculate the chi-squared test statistic (χ²)

If H0 were true, each day would receive one-fifth of the tickets. The expected value is therefore the same for every day:

E = 120 × 15 = 24

We then use the same formula as before and calculate one contribution per day:

χ² = Σ (O − E)²E

DayObserved (O)Expected (E)(O − E)² / E
Monday36246.00
Tuesday22240.17
Wednesday20240.67
Thursday18241.50
Friday24240.00
Total1201208.33

The six contributions add up to χ² = 8.33. Monday contributes most of it. The test needs expected values of at least 5, and here every one is 24.

Step 3: Find the p-value

For a goodness-of-fit test, the degrees of freedom are the number of categories minus one:

df = 5 − 1 = 4

Once four days’ counts are known, the fifth is fixed by the total of 120.

At a significance level of 5%, the critical value for 4 degrees of freedom is 9.49. Our statistic of 8.33 is below it.

The corresponding p-value is approximately 0.080. It was determined using Python. This means that if tickets really were spread evenly, a gap this large would appear in about 8 out of 100 weeks.

Step 4: Make a conclusion

The p-value of about 0.080 is above α = 0.05, so we fail to reject H0. This week’s data do not give enough evidence that tickets are spread unevenly.

That does not prove the load is even. Monday looks busy, but one week of data cannot separate a real pattern from chance. Collecting several weeks would give the test more to work with.

Coding

You do not have to calculate the statistic and the p-value by hand. The chisquare() function from Python’s scipy.stats package does both. It needs the observed and the expected values.

from scipy import stats

observed = [36, 22, 20, 18, 24]
expected = [24, 24, 24, 24, 24]

result = stats.chisquare(f_obs=observed, f_exp=expected)
print(f"chi-squared = {result.statistic:.2f}, p = {result.pvalue:.3f}")
# chi-squared = 8.33, p = 0.080

The output confirms our calculation. By default, the degrees of freedom are the number of categories minus one, which is 4 here. Because the p-value is above 0.05, we fail to reject H0.

Chi-Squared Test for Independence: A question about two categories

An online shop looks at its last 300 orders. For each order, it records two things: whether the customer is new or returning, and which delivery option they chose. Counting the orders gives us a table of observed counts.

Pickup stands out. Only 10 of 100 orders from new customers (10%) used pickup, compared with 70 of 200 orders from returning customers (35%).

But does this difference indicate an association between customer type and delivery choice, or could it simply be due to random variation?

The chi-squared test of independence helps us investigate this by comparing the observed counts with the counts we would expect if the two variables were unrelated.

Step 1: Identify the null and alternative hypotheses

We begin with two hypotheses:

  • H0 (null hypothesis): Customer type and delivery choice are independent. Both groups have the same distribution of delivery choices.
  • H1 (alternative hypothesis): Customer type and delivery choice are associated. The distribution of delivery choices differs between the two groups.

Step 2: Calculate the chi-squared test statistic (χ²)

The chi-squared statistic measures how much the observed counts differ from the expected values, the counts we would see if customer type and delivery choice were independent.

What we would expect if there were no relationship

If H0 were true, new and returning customers would choose delivery options in the same proportions.

Across all 300 orders, 150 (50%) used Standard, 70 (23.3%) used Express, and 80 (26.7%) used Pickup.

New customers placed 100 orders, one-third of the total. If they followed the same distribution, we would expect one-third of each delivery option’s orders to come from them: 50 Standard, 23.3 Express, and 26.7 Pickup orders.

We calculate the expected value for each cell using:

Expected value = row total × column totalgrand total

For example, the expected number of Pickup orders from new customers is:

E = 100 × 80300 = 26.7

Here, 100 is the total number of orders from new customers, 80 is the total number of Pickup orders, and 300 is the total number of orders.

We apply the same formula to all six cells to obtain the expected values in the table below. These are not predictions of actual orders; they represent the values we would expect under the assumption of independence.

Measuring the gap: the χ² statistic

To measure how far our observed counts differ from the expected values, we calculate a contribution for each cell and add them together.

χ² = Σ (O − E)²E

  • O = observed count: what actually happened.
  • E = expected value: what we would expect if the variables were independent.
  • (O − E)² = the squared difference between them.
  • Dividing by E scales the difference relative to the expected value.

Squaring prevents positive and negative differences from cancelling each other out. Dividing by E makes the size of a difference relative to the expected value matter.

For our table, the six cell contributions add up to χ² = 21.48.

New customers, Pickup1026.6710.42

The expected values are shown with two decimals here; the calculation uses the unrounded values.

Three numbers help explain where this value comes from:

  • Pickup, new customers: 10.42. We expected 26.7 orders but observed only 10. This cell contributes almost half of the total chi-squared statistic.
  • Pickup, returning customers: 5.21. We observed 70 orders instead of the expected 53.3.
  • The other four cells: 5.86 combined. Their differences contribute the remainder of the statistic.

How to read the χ² value

A χ² value of zero means the observed counts and expected values are identical. Larger values indicate greater discrepancies between them. To determine whether the discrepancy is statistically significant, we compare the statistic with a chi-squared distribution using the appropriate degrees of freedom.

Step 3: Find the p-value

Is a chi-squared statistic of 21.48 large? To answer that, we need the degrees of freedom, which determine the shape of the chi-squared distribution.

df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1)

Our table has 2 rows and 3 columns, so:

df = (2 − 1) × (3 − 1) = 2

Once the row and column totals are fixed, only two cell counts can vary independently. The remaining four are determined by those totals, giving us 2 degrees of freedom.

If H0 is true, the chi-squared statistic follows a chi-squared distribution with 2 degrees of freedom. This distribution is right-skewed and has no negative values. Because larger statistics indicate greater discrepancies between observed and expected values, the test looks at the right tail.

At a significance level of 5%, the critical value is 5.99 (you can look it up in a chi-squared table: find the row for 2 degrees of freedom and the column for 0.05, or calculate it with software). If our statistic exceeds this value, we reject H0.

Our statistic of 21.48 is far beyond that threshold. The corresponding p-value is approximately 0.000022. It was determined using Python. This means that such a large discrepancy would be extremely unlikely if customer type and delivery choice were independent.

We therefore reject H0 and conclude that the data provide strong evidence of an association between customer type and delivery choice.

Step 4: Make a conclusion

The p-value of approximately 0.000022 is far below our significance level of α = 0.05. We therefore reject H0 in favor of H1.

The data provide strong evidence that customer type and delivery choice are associated. New and returning customers do not choose delivery options in the same proportions.

What the test does not tell us

A significant result tells us that the observed differences are unlikely to be explained by random variation alone under the null hypothesis. It does not tell us how strong the association is, why it exists, or whether customer type causes a particular delivery choice.

To understand where the difference comes from, we can compare observed and expected values across individual cells. In our example, Pickup contributes most to the chi-squared statistic. To quantify the overall strength of the association, we can calculate Cramér’s V.

Coding

For the test of independence, scipy.stats offers chi2_contingency(). It only needs the observed table and calculates the expected values for you.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

observed = np.array([[60, 30, 10],
                     [90, 40, 70]])

chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(observed)

print(f"chi-squared = {chi2:.2f}, p = {p:.6f}, df = {dof}")
print(expected.round(1))
# chi-squared = 21.48, p = 0.000022, df = 2
# [[ 50.   23.3  26.7]
#  [100.   46.7  53.3]]

The function returns the χ² statistic, the p-value, the degrees of freedom and the expected values, in this order. They match our calculation.

One detail for 2 × 2 tables: with 1 degree of freedom, the function applies Yates’ correction by default, which makes small differences less likely to look significant. Our table is 2 × 3, so no correction is applied. For a 2 × 2 table with large expected values, you can switch it off with correction=False.

When not to use the chi-squared test

  • Very small expected values. As a rule of thumb, every expected value should be at least 5. Otherwise, merge categories or use Fisher’s exact test.
  • Percentages or averages. The test needs raw counts of observations.
  • Repeated observations. Each order or customer should be counted once. If the same customer appears many times, the observations are not independent.
  • Significant is not strong. With thousands of orders, even a tiny gap becomes significant. Cramér’s V measures the strength of the relationship from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect). For our table it is about 0.27, a small to moderate relationship.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“In our last 300 orders, new and returning customers chose their delivery options differently. The clearest difference is pickup: 10% of new customers used it, compared with 35% of returning customers. The chi-squared test indicates that this difference is unlikely to be explained by random variation alone.”

“The test tells us that customer type and delivery choice are associated, but it cannot tell us why. The business question is whether new customers are unaware of the pickup option or find it less convenient, and whether there is an opportunity to improve the experience.”

Key takeaway

The chi-squared test compares observed counts with the values we would expect under a specific null hypothesis.

  • Test of independence: Tests whether two categorical variables are associated.
  • Goodness-of-fit test: Tests whether the observed distribution of one categorical variable matches an expected distribution.

Both follow the same core logic: calculate the expected values, measure the differences using χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E, and evaluate the result using the appropriate chi-squared distribution and degrees of freedom.

A significant result provides evidence against the null hypothesis. It does not, by itself, establish causation or explain why the observed pattern occurred.
