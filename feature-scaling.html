Feature Scaling: Why Units Shouldn’t Decide Your Model

How standardization, normalization, and robust scaling put features on a fair footing.

A telecom company has two columns for each customer: tenure in months (1 to 72) and total charges in euros (about 100 to 8,000). It wants to find customers who behave alike. A customer with 24 months and €1,900 should look similar to one with 26 months and €2,700. But a distance-based model has no idea what a month or a euro is. It only sees numbers, and the bigger numbers win.

Feature scaling puts features on a comparable scale before the model sees them, so the units you happened to measure in don't decide the result. In this article we look at five scaling methods, when each one fits, and how to apply them without leaking information from the test set.

Why Scaling Matters, and Which Models Need It

Why units cause trouble

Take customer D, who has 24 months of tenure and €1,900 in total charges. Customer P has 60 months and €2,300, while customer Q has 26 months and €2,700.

Intuitively, Q looks more similar to D. Their tenure differs by only two months, compared with P’s 36-month difference.

A distance-based model doesn’t see it that way. It doesn’t understand what months or euros mean. It compares numerical values and measures how far apart two customers are with Euclidean distance: the straight-line distance between two points, the one you would measure with a ruler on a chart. Take the gap in each feature (the difference between the two customers), square it, add the squares, and take the square root.

d = √((tenure gap)² + (charges gap)²)

D to P: √(36² + 400²) = √161,296 ≈ 400

D to Q: √(2² + 800²) = √640,004 ≈ 800

The smaller the distance, the more similar the two customers look to the model, so the model picks P. The distance to P is almost the same as the euro gap alone: the 36 months barely add anything, because the differences in euros are numerically much larger than the differences in months. As a result, total charges dominate the distance calculation, making P appear closer to D.

Scaling addresses this problem by transforming each feature onto a comparable numerical scale before calculating distances. After Min-Max Scaling, the same gaps are measured on a 0 to 1 scale:

D to P: √(0.53² + 0.07²) ≈ 0.53

D to Q: √(0.03² + 0.14²) ≈ 0.14

Now Q is the closer customer, because the tenure gap counts as much as the charges gap.

Which models need scaling, and which don’t

A model needs scaling when it compares the size of numbers. Distance-based models are the clearest case, as Chart 1 shows. Other models are hurt in less visible ways. The table sums up which model families need scaling.

Model familyNeeds scaling?Why
Distance-based, such as K-Means Clustering and k-nearest neighborsYesDistances add up raw differences, so large units dominate.
Regularized and gradient-based, such as ridge, lasso and neural networksYesThe penalty and the learning steps treat features unequally when their units differ.
Support vector machines and PCAYesBoth rely on distances or variance, which depend on units.
Tree-based, such as a decision tree or random forestNoA split asks “is charges above €1,500?”, and rescaling doesn’t change who lands on each side.

Take a ridge model on the telecom data. The penalty shrinks every coefficient by the same rule. A coefficient for euros is naturally tiny and one for months is naturally larger, so the same penalty hits the two features unequally, only because of their units. See Variable Selection vs. Regularization: Managing Model Complexity for how that penalty works.

Scaling Methods

This section covers five methods. All of them are shown on the same example: five customers and one column, total charges. Customer A pays €500, B pays €1,000, C pays €1,500, D pays €2,000 and E pays €5,000. Customer E is a heavy spender, so the differences between the methods are easy to see.

Terms used in this section

  • Average (mean). All values added up and divided by how many there are. For our five customers: €2,000.
  • Standard deviation. The typical distance between a customer and the average. Small means customers are alike, large means they are spread out.
  • Median. The value of the customer in the middle when everyone is lined up from lowest to highest.
  • Outlier. A value far away from the rest, like customer E.

Standardization (Z-Score)

Standardization asks one question about every customer: how far above or below the average are you, counted in typical steps?

Heights show how this works. Someone who is 190 cm tall in a group that averages 175 cm is clearly tall. But how tall? That depends on how much heights usually vary. If most people are within 8 cm of the average, 15 cm above is almost two typical steps up. The size of a typical step is the standard deviation.

The same idea fixes the telecom problem. Average total charges are €2,000 and a typical step is about €1,581 (the population standard deviation, which is what scikit-learn uses). If tenure has a typical step of 24 months, a customer 24 months above the average tenure is one step up. Now euros and months are measured in the same unit, steps, so one step in tenure counts as much as one step in spending.

In words: take the value, subtract the average, and divide by the size of a typical step.

z = x − meanstandard deviation

Customer A gets z = (500 − 2,000) / 1,581 = −0.95. A spends almost one typical step less than the average customer.

A€500−0.95
B€1,000−0.63
C€1,500−0.32
D€2,0000.00
E€5,0001.90

E has z = 1.90, so E spends about 1.9 typical steps above the average. Negative values mean below average and 0 means exactly average. Standardization is the default choice for most models. Its weak spot is outliers, because a customer like E pulls both the average and the typical step.

Standardization does not make the data normal. It only moves the center to 0 and rescales the spread to 1. A skewed distribution stays skewed: if the original data leans to one side, the standardized data leans the same way. The bell shape in Chart 2 is only a stylized picture of a normal distribution.

In Python, scikit-learn does both steps in one call. The scaler computes the average and the typical step from the column and applies them:

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
import numpy as np

charges = np.array([[500], [1000], [1500], [2000], [5000]])
z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(charges)   # −0.95, −0.63, −0.32, 0.00, 1.90

Use it when: you aren’t sure which method to pick, or the model is k-means, KNN, SVM, PCA or a regularized model.

Min-Max Normalization

Min-max normalization squeezes a column onto a ruler from 0 to 1. The smallest value becomes 0, the largest becomes 1, and everyone else lands in between in proportion.

Place all customers on a ruler by spending. The lowest spender stands at the left end and the highest spender at the right end. A customer who spends a quarter of the way from lowest to highest stands at 0.25.

In words: subtract the smallest value, then divide by the distance from the smallest to the largest.

x′ = x − minmax − min

The smallest value is €500 and the largest is €5,000, so the distance is €4,500. Customer C gets (1,500 − 500) / 4,500 = 0.22. C is 22% of the way from the lowest to the highest spender.

CustomerTotal chargesMin-max value
A€5000.00
B€1,0000.11
C€1,5000.22
D€2,0000.33
E€5,0001.00

The catch: E defines the right end of the ruler, so A to D are squeezed into the first third. One extreme value is enough to flatten everyone else.

Min-Max Scaling guarantees the 0–1 range, but it does not fix outliers. One extreme value still decides where everyone else lands.

In Python (using the same charges column):

from sklearn.preprocessing import MinMaxScaler

mm = MinMaxScaler().fit_transform(charges)    # 0.00, 0.11, 0.22, 0.33, 1.00

Use it when: the model needs inputs between 0 and 1, as neural networks often do, and the column has no extreme outliers.

Max Absolute Scaling

Max absolute scaling divides every value by the biggest value in the column. Nothing else changes, in particular zero stays zero.

It turns amounts into shares of the biggest one. The biggest spender becomes 1, and someone who spends half as much becomes 0.5.

In words: divide each value by the largest absolute value, which is the largest value when you ignore minus signs.

x′ = xmax |x|

The largest value is €5,000, so customer B gets 1,000 / 5,000 = 0.20. B spends one fifth as much as the biggest spender.

CustomerTotal chargesMax absolute value
A€5000.10
B€1,0000.20
C€1,5000.30
D€2,0000.40
E€5,0001.00

Why does zero matter? Take a column like “international calls”, which is 0 for most customers. Methods that subtract something first would turn those zeros into other numbers and fill the table with non-zero values. Dividing keeps the zeros, so sparse data stays sparse. The weakness is the same as for min-max: one very large value squeezes the rest.

In Python:

from sklearn.preprocessing import MaxAbsScaler

ma = MaxAbsScaler().fit_transform(charges)    # 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 1.00

Use it when: your data has many zeros, such as counts of rarely used services.

Robust Scaling

Standardization uses the mean and standard deviation, which are sensitive to extreme values. In our example, customer E pulls the average up to €2,000, even though three of the five customers spend €1,500 or less.

Robust Scaling uses the median and interquartile range (IQR) instead. The median represents the middle value (€1,500), while the IQR measures the spread of the middle 50% of customers (€2,000 − €1,000 = €1,000).

The transformation subtracts the median and divides by the IQR:

Xscaled = X − MedianIQR

This centers the data around zero and makes it less sensitive to extreme values.

For customer E this gives (5,000 − 1,500) / 1,000 = 3.5. E sits 3.5 IQRs above the median. For customer A it gives (500 − 1,500) / 1,000 = −1.0, which is one IQR below.

CustomerTotal chargesRobust value
A€500−1.00
B€1,000−0.50
C€1,5000.00
D€2,0000.50
E€5,0003.50

The four typical customers are spread between −1.0 and 0.5, and E stands out at 3.5. You can still see that E is unusual, but E no longer squeezes everyone else.

Here is the proof. If E paid €50,000 instead of €5,000, A to D would still get −1.0, −0.5, 0.0 and 0.5, because the median and the middle half haven’t changed. Standardization would not be so stable: the average would jump to €11,000 and the typical step to about €19,500, so A to D would all land between about −0.54 and −0.46, almost on top of each other.

In Python:

from sklearn.preprocessing import RobustScaler

rob = RobustScaler().fit_transform(charges)   # −1.0, −0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 3.5

Use it when: your data has real outliers that you want to keep, such as a few very heavy spenders.

All three number lines run from −1.5 to 4, so you can compare how far the scaled values spread. Blue dots are customers A to D, red is the heavy spender E.

Standardization

z = (x − mean) / std

  • Centers on the mean
  • Unit one standard deviation
  • Outliers pull the mean and the std, so they distort the scale
  • Result for E 1.90; A to D between −0.95 and 0.00

Min-Max

(x − min) / (max − min)

  • Output range always 0 to 1
  • Unit the full range, smallest to biggest
  • Outliers very sensitive: E defines the maximum
  • Result for E 1.00; A to D between 0.00 and 0.33

Robust

(x − median) / IQR

  • Centers on the median
  • Unit the width of the middle 50%
  • Outliers hardly matter: E cannot move the median or the IQR
  • Result for E 3.50; A to D between −1.00 and 0.50

Rule of thumb: use Min-Max when you need a fixed 0 to 1 range and there are no extreme values, standardization as the general default, and Robust Scaling when real outliers stay in the data.

Unit Vector Scaling

The first four methods scale features column by column. Unit Vector Scaling works differently: it normalizes each row (each customer) independently.

It preserves the proportions between features but removes the overall magnitude. Think of each customer as an arrow: its direction represents the usage mix, while its length represents total usage. Unit Vector Scaling gives every arrow a length of 1 without changing its direction.

The length is calculated using the Euclidean norm:

‖X‖2 = √(x1² + x2² + ⋯ + xn²)

Each feature is then divided by this length:

Xscaled = X‖X‖2

Consider two customers of a SaaS product, using it for reports and exports:

  • Customer X: a small team that creates 30 reports and exports 20 files per month.
  • Customer Y: a large team that creates 60 reports and exports 40 files per month.

Their original usage differs, but their proportions are identical. Both produce the same normalized vector: (0.83, 0.55).

CustomerReports / exportsLengthUnit vector
X30 / 2036.060.83 / 0.55
Y60 / 4072.110.83 / 0.55
Z20 / 5053.850.37 / 0.93

Customer Z, a team that creates 20 reports and exports 50 files, has a different mix, so its arrow points in a different direction and its unit vector is different.

Unit Vector Scaling is useful when the relative proportions matter more than the total magnitude, for example, when comparing document vectors using cosine similarity. It is the wrong choice for ordinary features such as tenure and charges, because it would treat a €30 customer and a €300 customer as identical.

In Python, scikit-learn’s Normalizer works on rows. Its default is exactly this length-1 rule:

from sklearn.preprocessing import Normalizer

usage = np.array([[30, 20], [60, 40], [20, 50]])   # reports, exports (per month)
unit = Normalizer(norm="l2").fit_transform(usage)  # (0.83, 0.55), (0.83, 0.55), (0.37, 0.93)

Use it when: only the mix of features matters, not their size.

Choosing the Right Scaling Method

The right method depends on your data and your model. This table sums up where each one fits and what to watch for.

MethodBest whenMain risk
StandardizationDefault for k-means, KNN, SVM, PCA and regularized modelsOutliers pull the mean and standard deviation
Min-maxBounded inputs are needed, with no extreme outliersOne outlier squeezes everyone else
Max absoluteSparse data with many zerosSame outlier problem as min-max
RobustOutliers are real and should stayValues aren’t bounded, outliers stay large
Unit vectorOnly the mix of features mattersErases the size of each customer

A quick way to decide:

  • Outliers distort the column? Use robust scaling.
  • Need values between 0 and 1? Use min-max, after checking for outliers.
  • Sparse data with many zeros? Use max absolute scaling.
  • Comparing customers by their mix, not their size? Use unit vector scaling.
  • None of the above? Start with standardization.

Data Leakage: Fit on Training Data Only

Imagine you have 1,000 telecom customers. Before training your machine learning model, you standardize their features and then split the dataset into training and test sets.

This order creates a subtle problem: the mean and standard deviation used for scaling were calculated from all 1,000 customers, including those in the test set.

Although the model has not seen the test customers’ target values, information about their feature distributions has already influenced the preprocessing. This is called data leakage. It can make the model’s evaluation less reliable and give an overly optimistic impression of how well it performs on genuinely unseen data.

The correct approach is to split the data first, then learn the scaling parameters from the training set only. Apply those same parameters to both datasets:

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

scaler = StandardScaler()

# Learn scaling parameters from training data
X_train_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)

# Apply the same parameters to test data
X_test_scaled = scaler.transform(X_test)

Notice the difference between fit_transform() and transform():

  • fit_transform(): Calculates the mean and standard deviation from the training data, then standardizes it.
  • transform(): Uses the previously learned parameters to standardize the test data. It does not calculate new ones.

This distinction also matters for Min-Max Scaling. Suppose the training data contains ages between 20 and 60. A new customer who is 70 years old would receive a scaled value of 1.25, rather than a value between 0 and 1.

That is perfectly normal. The scaling parameters are based on the training data, so new observations can fall outside the original range. Do not refit the scaler just to force them back into it.

The takeaway: Split first, fit on training data, and transform the test data using the same parameters. This principle applies not only to scaling but also to other preprocessing techniques, such as imputation and feature selection.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“Before grouping our customers, we rescaled tenure and total charges. Tenure is measured in months, up to 72, while total charges run into the thousands of euros. Without rescaling, the model would have grouped customers almost only by how much they spend.

After rescaling, one typical step above average in tenure counts as much as one typical step above average in spending. In our test, a customer who had been with us 60 months stopped being called similar to one with 24 months just because their spending matched.

We learned the scaling from the training customers only, so our test results reflect how the model will treat new customers. The question we can now answer is: which customers behave alike in both how long they stay and how much they spend?”

Key Takeaways

Key takeaway

Feature scaling puts features on a comparable scale so that units don’t decide the result. Distance-based, regularized and gradient-based models need it, tree-based models don’t. Standardization is a good default, robust scaling copes with outliers, and every method must be fitted on training data only.
