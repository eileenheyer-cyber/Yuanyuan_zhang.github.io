Feature Scaling: Why Units Shouldn’t Decide Your Model

How standardization, normalization, and robust scaling put features on a fair footing.

Introduction

Imagine a telecom company that wants to find customers who behave alike. Two columns describe each customer: tenure in months (1 to 72) and total charges in euros (about 100 to 8,000). A customer with 24 months of tenure and €1,900 in charges should look similar to one with 26 months and €2,700. But a distance-based model has no idea what a month or a euro is. It only sees numbers, and the bigger numbers win.

Feature scaling puts features on a comparable scale before the model sees them, so the units you happened to measure in don't decide the result. In this article we look at five scaling methods, when each one fits, and how to apply them without leaking information from the test set.

Which Models Need Scaling (and Which Don’t)

A model needs scaling when it compares the size of numbers. Chart 1 is the clearest case: distance adds up raw differences, so a feature with large numbers dominates. Other models are hurt in less visible ways.

Model familyNeeds scaling?Why
Distance-based, such as K-Means Clustering and k-nearest neighborsYesDistances add up raw differences, so large units dominate.
Regularized and gradient-based, such as ridge, lasso and neural networksYesThe penalty and the learning steps treat features unequally when their units differ.
Support vector machines and PCAYesBoth rely on distances or variance, which depend on units.
Tree-based, such as a decision tree or random forestNoA split asks “is charges above €1,500?”, and rescaling doesn’t change who lands on each side.

Take a ridge model on the telecom data. The penalty shrinks every coefficient by the same rule. A coefficient for euros is naturally tiny and one for months is naturally larger, so the same penalty hits the two features unequally, only because of their units. See Variable Selection vs. Regularization: Managing Model Complexity for how that penalty works.

Scaling Methods

All five methods below are shown on the same example: five customers and one column, total charges. Customer E is a heavy spender, so the differences between the methods are easy to see.

Standardization (Z-Score)

Standardization subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation. Afterwards the column has a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

z = x − meanstandard deviation

For the five customers, the mean is €2,000 and the standard deviation is about €1,581 (the population standard deviation, which is what scikit-learn uses). Customer A gets z = (500 − 2,000) / 1,581 = −0.95.

A z-value says how many standard deviations a customer sits above or below the average. E has z = 1.90, so E spends about 1.9 typical steps above the average customer. This is what makes features comparable: one typical step above average in tenure counts as much as one in spending. Standardization is the default choice for most models. Its weak spot is outliers, because they pull the mean and the standard deviation.

Min-Max Normalization

Min-max normalization squeezes the column into the range 0 to 1, with the smallest value at 0 and the largest at 1.

x′ = x − minmax − min

The minimum is €500 and the maximum is €5,000, so the range is €4,500. Customer C gets (1,500 − 500) / 4,500 = 0.22.

The value says where a customer sits between the lowest and the highest spender. Use it when a model needs bounded inputs, as neural networks often do. The catch is visible in the table: E defines the maximum, so the other four customers are squeezed into 0 to 0.33. One extreme value is enough to flatten everyone else.

Max Absolute Scaling

Max absolute scaling divides every value by the largest absolute value in the column. It doesn’t shift the data, so zeros stay zeros.

x′ = xmax |x|

The largest absolute value is €5,000, so customer B gets 1,000 / 5,000 = 0.20.

Values end up between −1 and 1. Because nothing is shifted, a column full of zeros stays full of zeros, which makes this method a good fit for sparse data, such as counts of rarely used services. It shares the weakness of min-max scaling: one large value squeezes the rest.

Robust Scaling

Robust scaling subtracts the median and divides by the interquartile range (IQR), the spread of the middle 50% of customers. Both numbers ignore extreme values.

x′ = x − medianQ3 − Q1

The median is €1,500, Q1 is €1,000 and Q3 is €2,000, so the IQR is €1,000. Customer E gets (5,000 − 1,500) / 1,000 = 3.5.

The four typical customers are spread from −1.0 to 0.5, and E stands out at 3.5 instead of dragging the scale. This is the method to choose when outliers are real and you want to keep them, for example a few very heavy spenders, without letting them distort everyone else’s values.

Unit Vector Scaling

The first four methods work on a column. Unit vector scaling works on a row: it divides each customer’s feature values by the length of that customer’s vector, so every customer ends up with length 1.

x′ = x‖x‖, where ‖x‖ = √(x1² + x2² + …)

Take monthly spend on two services, both in euros. Customer X spends €30 on voice and €20 on data, so ‖X‖ = √(30² + 20²) = 36.06 and the unit vector is (0.83, 0.55). Customer Y spends exactly twice as much, €60 and €40, so ‖Y‖ = 72.11 and the unit vector is the same.

CustomerVoice / data spendUnit vector
X€30 / €200.83 / 0.55

The result says how a customer splits spending, not how much the customer spends. That is the right question when only the mix matters, as with word counts in text or usage profiles compared by direction. It is the wrong choice for ordinary features such as tenure and charges, because it would treat a €30 customer and a €300 customer as identical.

Choosing the Right Scaling Method

The right method depends on your data and your model. This table sums up where each one fits and what to watch for.

MethodBest whenMain risk
StandardizationDefault for k-means, KNN, SVM, PCA and regularized modelsOutliers pull the mean and standard deviation
Min-maxBounded inputs are needed, with no extreme outliersOne outlier squeezes everyone else
Max absoluteSparse data with many zerosSame outlier problem as min-max
RobustOutliers are real and should stayValues aren’t bounded, outliers stay large
Unit vectorOnly the mix of features mattersErases the size of each customer

A quick way to decide:

  • Outliers distort the column? Use robust scaling.
  • Need values between 0 and 1? Use min-max, after checking for outliers.
  • Sparse data with many zeros? Use max absolute scaling.
  • Comparing customers by their mix, not their size? Use unit vector scaling.
  • None of the above? Start with standardization.

Fit on Training Data Only

Suppose you standardize all 1,000 telecom customers first and split them into training and test sets afterwards. The mean and standard deviation then include the test customers, so the training step has already seen part of the test data. The leak is small for scaling, but it is real: test scores look slightly better than the model will do on new customers.

The rule is simple. Learn the scaling numbers (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum) on the training customers only, then apply those same numbers to the test customers.

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_train_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)   # learn mean and std on training data
X_test_scaled = scaler.transform(X_test)         # reuse them, never refit

With this setup, a test customer can fall outside the range seen in training, for example a min-max value above 1. That is expected and not a bug: it tells you the new customer is more extreme than anyone the model learned from. Columns that are already 0 or 1, such as the result of One-Hot Encoding: How to Make Categories Readable for Machine Learning, are usually left as they are. For a complete leakage-safe pipeline, see Feature Engineering: Turning Raw Data into Meaningful Features.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“Before grouping our customers, we rescaled tenure and total charges. Tenure is measured in months, up to 72, while total charges run into the thousands of euros. Without rescaling, the model would have grouped customers almost only by how much they spend.

After rescaling, one typical step above average in tenure counts as much as one typical step above average in spending. In our test, a customer who had been with us 60 months stopped being called similar to one with 24 months just because their spending matched.

We learned the scaling from the training customers only, so our test results reflect how the model will treat new customers. The question we can now answer is: which customers behave alike in both how long they stay and how much they spend?”

Key Takeaways

Key takeaway

Feature scaling puts features on a comparable scale so that units don’t decide the result. Distance-based, regularized and gradient-based models need it, tree-based models don’t. Standardization is a good default, robust scaling copes with outliers, and every method must be fitted on training data only.
