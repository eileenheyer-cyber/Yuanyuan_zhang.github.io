Probability

Normal Distribution

Most people have seen the bell curve before. Knowing what it looks like is not the same as knowing what it tells you. This post starts with how any continuous distribution works, then looks at the normal distribution and the empirical rule, and ends with a simple way to spot outliers. It builds on Random Variables & Probability Distributions and Four Common Discrete Distributions.

Probability density and probability

A probability function gives the probabilities for the possible outcomes of a random variable. There are two types:

  • Probability mass function (PMF). Describes discrete random variables.
  • Probability density function (PDF). Describes continuous random variables.

The graph of a PDF is a curve. On it, the x-axis shows the value of the variable you measure, and the y-axis shows the probability density. Probability density is not the same as probability.

As an example, take a random sample of cherry trees. Their heights are approximately normally distributed with a mean of 15 feet and a standard deviation of 2 feet. What is the probability that a randomly chosen tree measures exactly 15.1 feet? Essentially zero. A continuous variable has infinitely many possible values: 15.1, 15.175, 15.175245 and so on, so no single value carries a meaningful probability.

For continuous distributions it only makes sense to talk about intervals, such as the interval between 14.5 and 15.5 feet. The probability of an interval is the area under the curve over that interval. For this interval the area is about 0.20, so there is roughly a 20% chance that a tree falls between 14.5 and 15.5 feet.

In practice

Data professionals do not calculate these areas by hand. They use statistical software.

The normal distribution

The normal distribution is a continuous probability distribution that is symmetric about the mean and bell-shaped. It is also called the Gaussian distribution, after the German mathematician Carl Gauss, who first described its formula. It is the most common distribution in statistics because so many datasets follow a bell shape: height, weight, blood pressure, shoe size, test scores and more.

All normal distributions share these features:

  • Bell shape. A peak at the center and two sides that slope downward.
  • Mean at the center. The mean is also the peak: the most probable outcome. For the trees, that is 15 feet.
  • Symmetry. 50% of the data lies above the mean and 50% below.
  • Less likely further out. The further a value is from the mean, the lower its probability. The least probable outcomes are the very short and very tall trees.
  • Total area equal to 1. The area under the curve covers 100% of the possible outcomes.

The empirical rule

The values on a normal curve are spread in a regular pattern, based on their distance from the mean. This is the empirical rule, also known as the 68-95-99.7 rule:

RangeShare of valuesCherry trees (mean 15, SD 2)
Within 1 standard deviation68%13 to 17 feet
Within 2 standard deviations95%11 to 19 feet
Within 3 standard deviations99.7%9 to 21 feet

In other words, most trees (68%) measure between 13 and 17 feet, and almost all of them (99.7%) measure between 9 and 21 feet. The rule gives you a quick estimate of how the values in a large dataset are distributed, without any calculation. That saves time and helps you understand your data faster.

Z-scores: how far from the mean?

The empirical rule counts distance in standard deviations. A z-score gives you that distance for any single value. It tells you how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean:

z = (x − μ) / σx is the value, μ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation.

A positive z-score means the value is above the mean, a negative one means it is below, and a z-score of 0 means it is exactly the mean. For the cherry trees (mean 15 feet, standard deviation 2 feet):

Tree heightCalculationZ-scoreMeaning
15 feet(15 − 15) / 20Exactly average
17 feet(17 − 15) / 211 standard deviation above the mean
11 feet(11 − 15) / 2−22 standard deviations below the mean
21 feet(21 − 15) / 23At the edge of the 99.7% range

The z-score connects directly to the empirical rule: a z-score between −1 and 1 is inside the 68% range, between −2 and 2 inside the 95% range, and between −3 and 3 inside the 99.7% range.

Z-scores also let you compare values that are measured on different scales. Imagine a score of 85 on a test with a mean of 70 and a standard deviation of 10, and a score of 650 on another test with a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100. Both have a z-score of 1.5, so both results are equally far above their group's average.

Using the curve to find outliers

Knowing where a value sits on a normal distribution also helps you detect outliers, which are values that differ significantly from the rest of the data. Data professionals typically treat values more than 3 standard deviations below or above the mean as outliers. In z-score terms, that means a z-score above 3 or below −3.

Outliers deserve a closer look because some extreme values come from errors in data collection or data processing, and these false values can skew your results. A 40-foot cherry tree has a z-score of (40 − 15) / 2 = 12.5. That is far outside the 9 to 21 foot range, so it is more likely a typo than a record-breaking tree.

Outliers are not automatically wrong

The 3 standard deviation rule tells you which values to check, not which ones to delete. An extreme value can also be real. Decide after you know where it came from.

Z-scores in machine learning

Z-scores are not only a tool for describing data. In machine learning, converting a feature to z-scores is a standard preprocessing step called standardization. Every feature is rescaled to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, so features measured on very different scales become comparable.

Imagine a model that predicts customer churn from two features: age (roughly 18 to 80) and yearly spend (roughly 0 to 50,000). Without scaling, the spend values are hundreds of times larger than the age values, and many models would treat spend as far more important simply because its numbers are bigger. After standardization, a value of 1.5 means "1.5 standard deviations above average" for both features, and neither one dominates because of its unit.

Models where it is a good fit

Model typeWhy z-score scaling helps
k-nearest neighbors, k-meansThey compare points by distance. A feature with large numbers would dominate the distance calculation.
Support vector machinesThey also rely on distances between points, so the scale of each feature changes the result.
Linear and logistic regression with regularization (Ridge, Lasso)The penalty is applied to the coefficients, and coefficient size depends on the feature's scale. Standardizing makes the penalty fair across features.
Neural networks and other models trained with gradient descentFeatures on a similar scale usually make training faster and more stable.
Principal component analysis (PCA)PCA looks for directions of high variance. A feature with a large scale would take over the first components.

Where it makes no difference

Tree-based models, such as decision trees, random forests and gradient boosting, split the data on thresholds within one feature at a time. They do not compare features with each other, so scaling does not change their results.

Two things to watch

  • Calculate the mean and standard deviation on the training data only. Then apply those same two numbers to the test data. If you use the whole dataset, information from the test set leaks into training and your evaluation looks better than it really is.
  • Standardizing does not make data normal. It only shifts and rescales it, and the shape stays the same. Z-scores also depend on the mean and the standard deviation, which extreme values can distort. If your data has strong outliers, check them first or consider a more robust scaling method.

Rule of thumb

If a model works with distances or learns by gradient descent, standardize your features. If it works with splits, like a decision tree, you can skip it. When you are not sure, standardizing rarely hurts.

How to explain it to stakeholders

Stakeholders rarely need the formula. They need to know what the curve means for their decision. Here is how it could sound in a meeting:

“This curve shows us what a typical delivery time looks like. Most of our orders cluster around the average, and the further we move away from that center, the less often we see those delivery times.”

“For example, about 68% of deliveries fall within one standard deviation of the average, about 95% within two, and almost all within three. So if our average delivery time is 4 days, we can expect roughly 95% of orders to arrive within the corresponding two-standard-deviation range.”

“The business question is therefore not ‘What is the formula?’ but ‘What range of delivery times should we expect, and when does a delivery become unusual enough that we should investigate it?’”

Be careful with "95%"

A 95% range describes the data you expect to see, not a guarantee. One in 20 values will still fall outside it, and that is normal. Say so, so that nobody reads a single value outside the range as proof that something is broken.

Key takeaway

The normal distribution tells you what is typical, what is rare and when a value deserves a second look, and the z-score gives you one number to measure it with. As a data professional you will use it to find patterns in many kinds of datasets, and it is also the foundation for more advanced methods such as hypothesis testing and regression analysis.
