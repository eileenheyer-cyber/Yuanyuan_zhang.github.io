NordHome Retail Analytics · Case study

Turning messy retail data into a reliable foundation for business decisions.

NordHome is a fictional European online retailer. I built this project end to end: from intentionally messy raw data, through data quality checks, dimensional modelling and exploratory analysis, to a four-page Power BI report. The dashboard is only the last layer — most of the work, and most of the thinking, happens before it.

Business context

A retailer that needs to know whether its growth is real.

NordHome, founded in 2018, sells home décor, kitchen essentials, beauty products, lifestyle goods and curated gift sets across ten European markets — DACH, the Nordics, Benelux, France and Poland. Orders come in through the website, a mobile app, marketplaces and by phone.

The data covers January 2021 to June 2024. Because 2024 is only a half-year, everything is read as H1 2024 against H1 2023.

The questions behind the analysis

  1. 01Is NordHome growing — and can I trust that growth?
  2. 02How much revenue leaks away through cancellations, returns and refunds?
  3. 03Are customers coming back, or is the business mostly acquiring one-time buyers?
  4. 04Which marketing channels actually convert, and where should budget shift?

Data cleaning

The data quality challenge

The difficult part wasn’t simply loading the data. I first had to determine which records could be trusted, which needed correction and which needed to be flagged.

496 orders that point to customers who don’t exist raw_orders · 31,465 rows
1,835 returns for products missing from the catalogue — 30.1% of all returns raw_returns · 6,097 rows
471 duplicate payment rows — and 1,930 payments with no method raw_payments · 31,936 rows
379 negative quantities on order lines — plus 241 zero prices raw_order_items · 75,473 rows

Also in the raw layer: every order_id appeared twice · ~18 spelling variants across 7 payment methods · 105 returns dated before the order · 22 negative refunds · invalid emails and mixed date formats.

How I decided what to do

  1. 01Detect — Find the issue.
  2. 02Count — How many rows are affected?
  3. 03Assess — What would it do to the business numbers?
  4. 04Decide — Clean, standardise, flag, map to unknown — or exclude. And write down why.

Ghost records are kept, not deleted

An order whose customer can’t be found is still a real sale. So instead of deleting these rows, I mapped them to an “unknown” key (−1): the totals stay correct, and analyses that need a real customer or product can still exclude them. Messy rows are never silently dropped.

Found later, by questioning a number that looked wrong

228 corrupted quantities

A suspiciously high average order value led me to quantities of up to 1,996. Back-solving from the line total recovered the true quantity for 216 rows.

A date table that quietly stretched

A downstream defect pushed the date range to Dec 2024 and silently broke every year-over-year measure. I fixed the range to the real Jan 2021 – Jun 2024 window.

148 people registered twice

91% had real orders under both IDs. Deleting would lose history, merging would break keys — so I kept both and added a canonical customer key.

Data modelling

Modeling the business

I used a Kimball-style star schema: four fact tables that record what happens, surrounded by six dimensions that describe it.

Before building the fact tables, I asked a simple question: What does one row actually represent?

That answer is called the grain, and I wrote it down for every table before building it. It decides which joins are safe, which measures can be added up, and where the same value might be counted twice.

Analytical problem-solving

A problem I had to solve

Problem

On my first version of the revenue-leakage chart, refunds looked like the biggest source of lost revenue: €142K.

Investigation

Three loss-rate measures had been written at different times against different bases — one divided by Net Revenue, another by Gross Sales Revenue. And the refund figure came from the returns table, which also holds refunds booked against cancelled and returned orders. The same money sat in more than one bucket.

Solution

I rebuilt all three on one base — Gross Order Value — with mutually exclusive numerators, all taken from order status. Order value now splits cleanly into retained, cancelled, returned and refunded.

Lesson

A reliable dashboard starts with a reliable data model. A wrong denominator isn’t cosmetic — it can change the decision an executive makes.

Exploratory data analysis

Asking questions before building the dashboard

Before the dashboard, I explored the data in Python: 25 charts across six business areas, each following the same pattern — question, chart, finding, interpretation, limitation. Three examples:

The question

How much order value do cancellations, returns and refunds take away?

What I found

About 17% of potential order value every year (16.5–17.4%) — a stable band with no improving trend. Cancellations are consistently the largest part.

Limit: This is operational impact, not cash loss: actual cash refunds are a much smaller 2.6%.

The question

Does the “average customer” describe the customer base?

What I found

Not quite. 151 of 7,969 customers (1.9%) spend above €6,790, up to €11,428 — a small high-value group that the average hides.

Limit: Unknown customers and returned, refunded or cancelled value are excluded.

The question

Do return rates depend on the sales channel or on the market?

What I found

Neither alone. Across 40 channel × country combinations, rates range from 16.0% to 22.8% (average 19.0%), highest for Marketplace in the Netherlands.

Limit: A modest signal: each cell is a small sample (~769 orders), so extreme cells may be noise.

The Python analysis and the Power BI report were built on different states of the data (some dashboard fields were regenerated later), so their numbers aren’t meant to be compared line by line.

Business insight

What the data revealed

Four findings from the Power BI report (H1 2024 vs. H1 2023) — each with what it means and why the business would care.

1 of 3 drivers explains the revenue decline
What it means

Fewer customers were roughly offset by more frequent purchases. Falling order value alone drove the year-over-year decline.

Why it matters

H2 initiatives should target basket size — bundles, upsell prompts, minimum-order incentives — not acquisition.

€115.6K lost to cancellations — the biggest leak
What it means

Cancelled order value beats returned (€90.0K) and refunded (€85.3K). Refunds only looked biggest because of a denominator error.

Why it matters

Cancellations are where an audit pays off first.

197–254 customers lost per month vs. 104–140 gained
What it means

Churn outpaces new customers in every month and every market. In the Nordics: 802 retained vs. 1,674 churned.

Why it matters

Retention budget should come before acquisition budget, starting in the Nordics and DACH.

6–8× better ROI for Display and Pinterest than Influencer
What it means

Influencer has the highest cost per acquisition (€28.21) and the lowest ROI (184.9%).

Why it matters

Moving spend from Influencer to Display and Pinterest is the most direct budget lever in the report.

Reflection

What I learned

This project changed how I think about analytics. The dashboard is the visible part of the work, but most of the real thinking happens before it: understanding the business, defining the grain, checking relationships, writing down assumptions and deciding how imperfect data should be handled.

Some of my best lessons came from numbers that looked fine and weren’t. A refund figure that ranked first rested on a different base than its neighbours. A date table quietly stretched to December 2024 and broke every year-over-year measure. I learned to ask of every number: would I still trust this if someone challenged it?

And not everything I can build should be shipped. I built concentration measures, checked them, and dropped them when the data showed no real pattern. A correct number that shows nothing is still clutter.

Next, I want to take the customer question further with a separate segmentation (RFM) project.
