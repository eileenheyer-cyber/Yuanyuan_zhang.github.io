Logistic Regression

What if the outcome you want to predict is not a number, but a yes or no?

A customer either cancels a subscription or stays. A transaction either defaults or does not. A patient either develops a condition or does not.

Logistic regression is designed for exactly these kinds of questions. Instead of predicting the outcome directly, it estimates the probability of an event occurring, giving us a value between 0 and 1 that can be used to make a yes-or-no decision.

In this post, we use a simple customer churn example with one predictor: the number of support tickets a customer opened last month. It builds on Simple Linear Regression and the framework introduced in Generalized Linear Models.

Why a Straight Line Fails for Yes/No Outcomes

Imagine a subscription company approaching a customer's renewal date. The company wants to know: Will this customer renew their subscription or cancel?

The answer has only two possible outcomes: stay or cancel. The company also knows how many support tickets the customer opened last month. Could this information help predict whether they will leave?

Ordinary linear regression is not designed for this kind of prediction.

Logistic regression addresses two limitations of ordinary linear regression:

  • Predictions stay within valid probability limits. A straight line can produce values below 0 or above 1, while logistic regression estimates probabilities between 0 and 1.
  • The relationship can be nonlinear. The probability of cancellation may rise slowly at first, increase sharply across a middle range, and then level off. Logistic regression captures this pattern with an S-shaped curve, also called a sigmoid curve (Chart 1).

From Probability to Odds to Log-Odds

The key idea is that logistic regression does not abandon the linear model. Instead, it models a straight-line relationship on a different scale: the log-odds scale.

The model does not start with a probability. For a customer with six support tickets, it first calculates the log-odds from the straight line, then converts them into odds, and finally into a probability:

  • Log-odds: −4 + 0.8 × 6 = 0.8
  • Odds: e0.8 ≈ 2.2
  • Probability: 2.2 ÷ (1 + 2.2) ≈ 0.69, or 69%

Reading these steps in the other direction is how we make sense of the result:

  • Probability: The chance of cancellation, between 0 and 1. Here, it is 0.69, or 69%.
  • Odds: The ratio of the probability of cancelling to the probability of staying (not cancelling). The odds are 0.69 ÷ 0.31 ≈ 2.2. In other words, the odds of cancelling are about 2.2 to 1.
  • Log-odds (logit): The natural logarithm of the odds. Here, ln(2.2) ≈ 0.8.

Odds = p1 − p Here, p is the probability of cancelling.

logit(p) = ln( p1 − p ) The logit, or log-odds, is the natural logarithm of the odds.

Why do we need the logit? We want a simple straight-line formula. But a probability is stuck between 0 and 1, so a straight line does not fit it. Unlike probabilities, the logit can take any value, from negative infinity to positive infinity, so logistic regression uses a straight line on this scale:

logit(p) = −4 + 0.8 × tickets For a customer with 6 tickets: −4 + 0.8 × 6 = 0.8.

In this example, −4 is the starting point: the logit for a customer with no tickets. The 0.8 means that each additional ticket raises the logit by 0.8.

A logit of 0.8 is hard to interpret, so the model converts it back into a probability with the logistic function, also known as the sigmoid function. This is the reverse of the steps above:

p = 11 + e−logit This compresses any logit value into a probability between 0 and 1, producing the S-shaped curve. For 6 tickets: 1 ÷ (1 + e−0.8) ≈ 0.69, or 69%.

When the log-odds equal zero, the odds are 1 to 1, corresponding to a probability of 50%. Positive log-odds indicate that cancellation is more likely than staying; negative log-odds indicate the opposite.

The important takeaway is that logistic regression is linear on the log-odds scale, not on the probability scale.

Reading the Coefficients as Odds Ratios

The coefficient 0.8 tells us how much the log-odds change with each additional support ticket. But log-odds are not particularly intuitive to interpret.

To make the coefficient easier to understand, we exponentiate it:

e0.8 ≈ 2.2 This gives us the odds ratio.

Each additional support ticket multiplies the odds of cancellation by approximately 2.2, so the odds more than double, assuming the model remains unchanged.

  • An odds ratio above 1 means the predictor increases the odds of the event.
  • An odds ratio below 1 means the predictor decreases the odds of the event.
  • An odds ratio equal to 1 means the predictor does not change the odds.

One important distinction: an odds ratio is not a change in probability.

For example, increasing the ticket count from four to five raises the predicted probability from approximately 31% to 50%, an increase of 19 percentage points. Increasing it from seven to eight raises the probability from approximately 83% to 92%, an increase of 9 percentage points.

The odds are multiplied by the same factor in both cases, but the probability changes by a different amount depending on the starting point.

This is why interpreting logistic regression coefficients requires distinguishing between odds and probabilities.

Turning a Probability into a Decision

Logistic regression produces a probability, but a business often needs a concrete action: should the retention team contact this customer or not?

This is where a decision threshold comes in.

A threshold converts the predicted probability into a classification. With a threshold of 0.5, customers with a predicted probability of at least 50% are flagged as likely to cancel.

In our example, a customer with eight tickets has a predicted probability of 92% and is flagged, while a customer with three tickets has a probability of approximately 17% and is not. With this particular model, a threshold of 0.5 corresponds to flagging customers with five or more tickets (Chart 3).

However, 0.5 is a common default, not a universal rule.

Suppose losing a customer costs the company ten times as much as making a retention offer. In that case, missing a customer who eventually cancels may be much more expensive than contacting someone who would have stayed. A lower threshold, such as 0.2, might therefore be more appropriate. In our example, that would flag customers with four or more tickets.

The ticket count where the predicted probability crosses a threshold is called the decision boundary. Customers on one side of it are flagged, and customers on the other side are not. For a threshold of 0.5, the boundary is at 5 tickets, where the log-odds equal 0 (Chart 4).

Lowering the threshold generally identifies more potential churners, but it also increases the number of customers contacted unnecessarily.

These mistakes have names:

  • False positive: The model flags a customer who would have stayed.
  • False negative: The model fails to flag a customer who later cancels.

The best threshold depends on the business objective and the relative costs of these errors. Model evaluation helps us compare these trade-offs, which I will cover in a separate post.

Assumptions and Common Pitfalls

Logistic regression makes different assumptions from linear regression. The most important ones to understand are:

  • Binary outcome. In standard binary logistic regression, the target has two possible outcomes, such as cancelled or stayed.
  • Independent observations. Each observation should be independent of the others, unless the model explicitly accounts for dependencies.
  • Linearity in the log-odds. The relationship between each continuous predictor and the log-odds should be appropriately modeled. This does not mean the relationship with probability must be linear.
  • Little to no multicollinearity. Predictors should not be excessively correlated with one another, as this can make individual coefficients difficult to estimate precisely.
  • Enough outcome events. The model needs sufficient cancellations relative to its complexity. A traditional rule of thumb is around ten events per predictor, but this is only a rough guideline; the required sample size depends on several factors.

Two additional pitfalls are worth watching for.

First, if very few customers cancel, a model that always predicts “stays” may achieve high accuracy while failing to identify the customers the business cares about. This is especially important when evaluating imbalanced datasets.

Second, if a predictor perfectly separates customers who cancel from those who stay, the model may struggle to estimate finite coefficients. This is known as complete separation.

Understanding these limitations helps us avoid treating a model's predictions as automatically reliable.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“In this example, customers with no support tickets last month have an estimated 2% probability of cancelling. At five tickets, the estimated probability reaches 50%, and at eight tickets, it rises to 92%. Each additional ticket multiplies the odds of cancellation by approximately 2.2.

Using a 0.5 threshold, the model flags customers with five or more tickets. The retention team can use this information to prioritize outreach, while evaluating whether this threshold makes sense given the costs of missed cancellations and unnecessary retention offers.”

This explanation connects the statistical model to the business question: Which customers should we prioritize to reduce churn?

Key takeaways

  • Logistic regression predicts the probability of a yes/no outcome, always between 0 and 1.
  • It is a straight line on the log-odds scale, converted back into an S-shaped curve.
  • e raised to a coefficient is the odds ratio. Here, 2.2 means the odds more than double per extra ticket. It is not a change in probability.
  • A threshold turns probabilities into decisions, and the best threshold depends on the business cost of each kind of mistake.
