OLS: How Does Linear Regression Find the Best-Fit Line?

Many different straight lines can be drawn through the same cloud of points, and most of them look reasonable. So how does regression decide which one is the best? It uses a simple rule: measure how far the line misses each point, add up those misses, and pick the line with the smallest total. This rule is called ordinary least squares (OLS). This post explains how it works and builds on Simple Linear Regression.

The question: which line is best?

Think of laying a ruler across a scatter of points. You can tilt it and shift it, and dozens of positions seem fine to the eye. To choose between them you need a rule that turns “fits well” into a number, so lines can be compared fairly. That number is built from the errors each line makes.

Take NordHome's five months from the last post: two people could draw two slightly different lines through them, and the eye alone cannot say who is right. The errors can.

Error Measurement and Residuals

A line makes an error at every point: the gap between what actually happened and what the line predicted. This gap is called the residual.

residual = observed value − predicted value = y − ŷ A positive residual means the point sits above the line, a negative one means it sits below.

For the €7k month, the line predicts 53.8 and the actual sales were 52, so the residual is −1.8. Chart 1 shows all five residuals of NordHome's fitted line as red segments.

The five residuals are +0.58, −1.16, +0.97, −1.76 and +1.37. They add up to zero. This is not luck: for OLS with an intercept, the residuals always sum to zero, because the line balances the points above and below it. It also means we cannot judge a line by the plain sum of its errors, since even a poor line can have errors that cancel out. So we square each residual first, which makes every error positive and punishes large misses more, and then add them up:

SSR = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² SSR is the sum of squared residuals. The smaller it is, the closer the line sits to the data. For NordHome's line, SSR ≈ 7.61.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Estimation

OLS finds the intercept and slope that make the SSR as small as possible. In other words, it finds the line with the least squared error, hence the name.

The coefficients are estimates calculated from a sample of data, here five months. The true relationship in the whole population is unknown, so we mark the estimates with a hat: β̂₀ and β̂₁. The previous post wrote them as b₀ and b₁; they are the same thing.

ŷ = β̂₀ + β̂₁x β̂₀ is the estimated intercept and β̂₁ is the estimated slope.

Practical example: trying different lines

Before using a formula, test it by hand. Here are three lines for NordHome's data:

LineSum of residualsSSR
ŷ = 15 + 5x+2088
ŷ = 20 + 4.5x+823.5
ŷ = 19.684 + 4.868x (OLS)07.61

The first line sits below every point, so all five residuals are positive and the SSR is large. The second is much closer, but its residuals are still mostly positive. The OLS line balances the errors and has the smallest SSR of any straight line. You could keep adjusting the slope and the intercept and watch the SSR change, but you would never get below 7.61. OLS does that search for you, with a formula instead of trial and error.

Estimating the coefficients

Trial and error works, but it is slow and never certain. The OLS formulas calculate the best intercept and slope directly from the means and sums of the observed values. With x̄ = 5.2 and ȳ = 45:

β̂₁ = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)² β̂₀ = ȳ − β̂₁x̄ For NordHome: β̂₁ = 111 / 22.8 ≈ 4.868 and β̂₀ = 45 − 4.868 × 5.2 ≈ 19.684.

The slope compares how x and y move together with how much x varies on its own. The line also always passes through the point (x̄, ȳ). In practice, software does all of this in a split second, but knowing what sits behind the numbers helps you read the output.

What comes next

OLS gives the best line for our sample, but the result is only trustworthy if certain model assumptions hold. The next posts cover those assumptions, and how to read the results with p-values and confidence intervals, which show how uncertain the estimates are.

Key takeaway

A residual is the observed value minus the predicted value. OLS finds the intercept and slope that minimise the sum of squared residuals, so no other straight line fits the sample better. The coefficients are estimates from a sample, written with hats, and software calculates them for you.
