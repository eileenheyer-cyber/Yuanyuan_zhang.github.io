Tree-based models

Gradient Boosting: How Each Tree Fixes the Errors Before It

AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting both improve their models by learning from previous mistakes, but they approach this process differently. AdaBoost increases the weights of misclassified observations, forcing later trees to focus on difficult cases. Gradient Boosting instead trains each new tree to correct the errors of the current model, gradually reducing the prediction error.

In this article we follow Gradient Boosting on a tiny example: eight customers and one question, will a first-time customer order again? You do not need any machine learning background. We start with the plain idea, do the calculations by hand, and finish with the settings that stop the model from overdoing it.

This is part of my series on boosting. If you haven’t read the first article yet, start with AdaBoost Explained: How Models Learn from Their Mistakes.

The idea in plain words

After a practice test you don’t re-read the whole book. You look at the questions you got wrong and study those. After the next test you do the same with the questions that are still wrong. Gradient Boosting builds a model in the same way: in rounds, and each round adds one small helper that only fixes what the model still gets wrong.

The helper is a small decision tree: a short list of yes/no questions about the data, such as “Was the first basket at most €42.50?”, that sorts customers into groups and gives each group one answer. One small tree is a weak predictor. Hundreds of them, each fixing the leftovers of the ones before, make a strong one.

Our example: eight customers

A shop wants to know which first-time customers will order again. The model gives every customer a probability between 0 and 1: 0 means “surely will not order again”, 1 means “surely will”. Why Gradient Boosting Uses the Logit Scale

A probability must always stay between 0 and 1. But a boosted model adds the corrections from many decision trees together. These corrections can be positive or negative, so adding them directly to a probability could produce an invalid result.

To avoid this problem, Gradient Boosting works with logits (raw scores) instead of probabilities.

From Probability to Logit

The logit transforms a probability into a score that can range from negative to positive infinity.

logit(p) = ln(p ÷ (1 − p))

Here, p is the predicted probability.

For example:

  • A probability below 0.50 produces a negative score.
  • A probability of 0.50 produces a score of 0.
  • A probability above 0.50 produces a positive score.

The model uses this score as its starting point and adds the corrections produced by successive trees.

New score = Previous score + Tree correction

From Logit Back to Probability

Once the tree corrections have been accumulated, the model converts the final score back into a probability using the sigmoid function:

p = 1 ÷ (1 + e−score)

This guarantees that the final prediction lies between 0 and 1.

Round 1: start, measure, correct

Step 1: Start Neutral and Measure the Errors

The model has not seen any customer data yet, so it predicts the same probability for everyone. Four of our eight customers ordered again, which is half, so the predicted probability is 50%, which is 0.5. On the logit scale this is a score of 0.

Now we check how wrong that predicted probability was for each customer. This difference is called the residual:

residual = what really happened − what the model predicted

What really happened is 1 if the customer ordered again and 0 if not.

  • Customers 4, 5, 7, 8 ordered again: 1 − 0.5 = +0.5. The predicted probability was too low.
  • Customers 1, 2, 3, 6 did not: 0 − 0.5 = −0.5. The predicted probability was too high.

A positive residual means “the model should be more optimistic about this customer”, and a negative one means “more cautious”. The next tree learns from the customer data which customers belong in which group.

A higher score always corresponds to a higher predicted probability.

The key idea: The logit scale gives Gradient Boosting room to add positive and negative corrections freely. The sigmoid function then translates the accumulated score into an interpretable probability.

A higher score always means a higher probability.

Step 2: Fit a Small Tree to the Residuals

Now comes the helper. The tree has one job: predict each customer’s residual from the customer data. In our tiny table the only information is the first basket, so the tree looks for the best yes/no question about it.

The best question is “Is the first basket at most €42.50?”. It is the best because it sorts customers into two groups whose residuals look alike. The three small-basket customers (1 to 3) all have −0.5, so the predicted probability was too high for all of them. Among the five larger-basket customers (4 to 8), four have +0.5 and only customer 6 has −0.5, so the predicted probability was mostly too low.

Each group now needs one number: how much to change the score of everyone in it. We get it by dividing the total residual of the group by p × (1 − p), added up over the same customers. In plain words: the residual is an error in probability, but the model adds up scores, so we have to translate. p × (1 − p) tells how fast the probability changes when the score changes. For a customer with a predicted probability of 0.5 it is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.

  • Small baskets (customers 1 to 3): total residual −1.5, divided by 0.75 (three times 0.25), gives −2.0.
  • Larger baskets (customers 4 to 8): total residual +1.5, divided by 1.25 (five times 0.25), gives +1.2.

Read the two numbers as instructions: lower the score for small baskets, raise it for larger ones. Dividing by p × (1 − p) is a first taste of the curvature idea that XGBoost builds on, explained in XGBoost: Gradient Boosting That Knows How Far to Step.

Step 3: Update the Prediction with the Learning Rate

We could add the tree’s numbers to the score in full, but that would be risky: one tree is only a rough helper, and a big jump can overshoot. So we take only a part of each step. The part is called the learning rate, written η (eta), a number between 0 and 1:

new score = old score + η × correction

For this example we use η = 0.5, a large value chosen only to keep the arithmetic easy to follow. The old score was 0 for everyone:

  • Customers 1 to 3: 0 + 0.5 × (−2.0) = −1.0, which is a probability of 0.27.
  • Customers 4 to 8: 0 + 0.5 × 1.2 = +0.6, which is a probability of 0.65.

Before, everyone had 0.5 (all numbers from here on are rounded to two decimals, and every step is calculated from the rounded numbers of the step before). Now the model is less hopeful about small baskets and more hopeful about larger ones, which is the right direction for seven of the eight customers. Only customer 6 (€70, did not order again) moved the wrong way, because a €70 basket looks like the larger baskets.

Round 2, round 3 and so on

Round 2 does not start from 0.50 again. It starts from the predicted probabilities that tree 1 produced: 0.27 for customers 1 to 3 and 0.65 for customers 4 to 8. Each tree corrects the model as it is after the trees before it. So the residuals change too. A residual is still what happened minus the predicted probability, and what happened is 1 if the customer ordered again and 0 if not:

  • Customers 1 to 3 did not order again (what happened = 0): 0 − 0.27 = −0.27
  • Customers 4, 5, 7, 8 ordered again (what happened = 1): 1 − 0.65 = +0.35
  • Customer 6 did not order again (what happened = 0): 0 − 0.65 = −0.65. This is the biggest error left, because the model now expects this customer to order again.

The second tree looks for the best question for these new residuals. It finds “Is the first basket at most €75?”. That puts customers 1 to 6 in one group and the two largest baskets, customers 7 and 8, in the other, because those two still get a predicted probability that is too low.

For every group we need two totals. The first is the total residual, the sum of the residuals of the group. It shows how wrong the group is overall. The second is the sum of p × (1 − p), which translates that error from probability into a change of score, as in Step 2. Dividing the first by the second gives the correction for the whole group.

Why it is called gradient boosting

So far a residual was simply what happened minus the predicted probability. There is a second way to see it, and it explains the name.

The model needs a way to measure how bad a prediction is. This measure is called the loss: small for a good prediction, large for a bad one. For repeat orders the usual choice is the log loss, and Chart 3 shows it for one customer who did order again. The horizontal axis is the model’s score for this customer, the vertical axis is the loss.

Log Loss

The log loss measures how bad a predicted probability is for one customer. For a customer who ordered again it is −ln(p), and for a customer who did not it is −ln(1 − p). The logarithm punishes confident mistakes much harder than cautious ones.

For a customer who ordered again: a predicted probability of 0.88 gives a loss of 0.13, 0.50 gives 0.69, and 0.12 gives 2.12. A model that is confident and right pays almost nothing, and a model that is confident and wrong pays a lot.

Chart 3 shows the loss for one customer who did order again. The horizontal axis is the model’s score for this customer, with the matching probability below each tick. The vertical axis is the loss. The orange dot is where the model stands now: a score of 0, a predicted probability of 0.50 and a loss of −ln(0.50) = 0.69. The curve falls to the right, because a higher score means a higher probability of a repeat order, and that is the right answer for this customer.

Think of the curve as a hill and of the model as a hiker who wants to walk down to the lowest point. At the dot, the hill slopes downward to the right. The steepness of that slope is called the gradient.

For the log loss the gradient is p − y. For this customer it is 0.50 − 1 = −0.50. The minus sign says that the hill goes down to the right, so the model has to raise the score.

The model therefore moves against the gradient, which means it uses the gradient with its sign flipped, the negative gradient: y − p = 1 − 0.50 = +0.50. This is exactly the residual from Step 1. The residual tells the model in which direction downhill lies. It tells the direction, not yet how far to go. XGBoost: Gradient Boosting That Knows How Far to Step answers that question.

This gives the method its name. A tree fitted to the residuals learns the downhill direction for all customers at once, and adding it is one step downhill on the loss. Gradient stands for the slope that shows the direction, and boosting for adding many small helpers one after another. The same recipe works for other losses, for example the squared error when predicting a number.

Stopping the model from overdoing it

Boosting keeps correcting mistakes, and that is also its risk. At first the trees learn real patterns, such as small baskets rarely coming back. After a while the only mistakes left are random noise. A model that keeps chasing them memorizes the training customers instead of learning what holds for new ones, like a student who memorizes last year’s exam answers: perfect on old questions, lost on new ones. This is called overfitting.

How can we see overfitting happen? By testing the model on customers it has not seen. We split the 5,000 customers into 3,750 for training, the customers the trees are built from, and 1,250 for testing, the customers kept back to check the result. The accuracy on the training customers keeps rising with every tree, because the model can always memorize a little more. The accuracy on the test customers rises only while the model learns real patterns, and then it stops rising or falls. The number of trees at that point is the right one.

The learning rate η controls how much of each tree’s correction is applied, and it works together with the number of trees. With a large η (for example 0.5), every tree makes a big correction, so the model learns fast but also memorizes the training data quickly. With a small η (for example 0.1), many more trees are needed, but the model approaches the pattern gently. With all five features and a tree depth of 3, the two settings look like this:

The learning rate is one safeguard. Three more work alongside it, and each one stops the model from overdoing it in a different way:

  • Shallow trees. The depth of a tree is the number of questions in a row. Our trees have a depth of 1: one question about the first basket. This is the point of boosting, many small helpers instead of one big one. A deeper tree, with 3 to 5 questions, can combine features, for example a discount code together with late delivery, but it also fits noise faster. Trees with one question are the stumps from AdaBoost Explained: How Models Learn from Their Mistakes.
  • Subsampling. Each tree sees only a random share of the customers, for example 80%. The trees then differ a little from each other, as the random samples do in Random Forest: Why Many Trees Beat One, and no single tree can memorize the whole table.
  • Early stopping. The model finds this point by itself. Part of the training customers is set aside as a check set and is not used to build trees. After every tree the model is scored on this check set. Once the score has not improved for a number of trees, training stops. The test customers stay untouched until the final result.

Gradient Boosting in Python

With the customer table prepared and split into training and test data, training a gradient boosting model takes a few lines:

from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier

gb = GradientBoostingClassifier(
    n_estimators=500, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, subsample=0.8,
    validation_fraction=0.2, n_iter_no_change=20, random_state=42)
gb.fit(X_train, y_train)

Each setting in the code controls one of the safeguards from the previous section:

SettingWhat it doesValue
n_estimatorsThe most trees the model may build. Early stopping usually ends training sooner500
learning_rateHow much of each tree’s correction is added0.1
max_depthThe most questions in a row in one tree3
subsampleThe share of the training customers each tree sees0.8
validation_fractionThe share of the training customers set aside as the check set0.2
n_iter_no_changeTraining stops when this many trees in a row bring no improvement on the check set20
random_stateFixes the random choices, so the same code gives the same model every time42

Early stopping ends training after 107 trees. On the 1,250 test customers the model is right for 79.4%, compared with 63.4% for always predicting “no repeat order”. Its AUC is 0.865. The AUC is a score from 0.5 (no better than chance) to 1 (perfect) that rates how well the model ranks customers who will order again above those who will not. It is explained in Common Logistic Regression Metrics.

How to explain it to stakeholders

“We built the model in small steps. The first step is a rough starting prediction: about half of our first-time customers order again. Then we add simple rules one at a time, for example ‘customers whose first basket is under €42.50 are less likely to come back’. Each new rule is only allowed to fix what the earlier rules still get wrong.

We stop adding rules when the model stops improving on customers it has never seen, so it doesn’t just memorize the past. On 1,250 test customers it was right 79% of the time, compared with 63% for always predicting ‘no repeat order’. So which new customers deserve a follow-up offer?”

Key takeaway

Gradient boosting starts with one neutral prediction and adds small trees one after another. Each tree learns the errors that are left, the residuals, and only a small part of its correction, set by the learning rate, is added. Small steps, shallow trees and early stopping keep the model from memorizing noise.
