Common Logistic Regression Metrics

Imagine building a logistic regression model to predict customer churn. It achieves 85% accuracy. Sounds good, right?

But what if it misses many customers who are about to leave or incorrectly flags loyal customers as potential churners? Accuracy alone doesn't tell the whole story.

In this article, we'll explore common classification metrics, from the confusion matrix to precision, recall, ROC curves and AUC. Using a customer churn example, we'll see how these metrics work and how to choose the right one for a given business problem.

This article builds on Logistic Regression, where the churn model is introduced.

How Logistic Regression Makes a Decision

Before evaluating a logistic regression model, let's understand how it makes predictions.

Unlike linear regression, which predicts a continuous numerical value, logistic regression estimates the probability that an observation belongs to a particular class. For example, a customer churn model might estimate that a customer has an 80% probability of cancelling their subscription.

But a probability isn't yet a classification. To convert probabilities into binary predictions, we introduce a decision threshold. A common default is 0.5:

  • Probability ≥ 0.5 → predict churn.
  • Probability < 0.5 → predict no churn.

The threshold determines how the model translates probabilities into decisions. It is a setting we can adjust depending on our objectives.

  • Lowering the threshold to 0.3 means that more customers will be classified as potential churners. This may help us identify more customers who eventually leave, but it can also increase false alarms.
  • Raising the threshold to 0.7 makes the model more conservative about predicting churn. This may reduce false alarms, but it can also cause the model to miss customers who actually leave.

The key idea: the decision threshold influences the predictions, and the predictions determine the evaluation metrics.

Let's see how this works with a concrete example. Imagine our model evaluates 20 customers: 10 actually churned and 10 stayed. After applying a decision threshold of 0.5, the model produces the following results:

Customer outcomeNumber of customers
Correctly identified churners9
Churners incorrectly predicted to stay1
Customers incorrectly predicted to churn2
Correctly identified customers who stayed8

These four outcomes form the foundation of the confusion matrix.

The Confusion Matrix: Where the Metrics Come From

A confusion matrix compares a model's predictions with the actual outcomes. It shows not only how many predictions were correct, but also which types of mistakes the model made (Chart 1).

The four cells represent four possible outcomes:

  • True Positive (TP) = 9. The model predicted that nine customers would churn, and all nine actually churned.
  • False Negative (FN) = 1. One customer actually churned, but the model predicted that they would stay.
  • False Positive (FP) = 2. The model predicted that two customers would churn, but both stayed.
  • True Negative (TN) = 8. The model correctly predicted that eight customers would stay.

These outcomes reveal something that accuracy alone cannot show: a model can make different types of mistakes, and those mistakes may have different consequences. Missing a customer who is about to cancel might mean losing an opportunity to retain them. Incorrectly flagging a loyal customer might mean wasting resources on an unnecessary retention campaign. The confusion matrix makes these errors visible.

In Python, scikit-learn's confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred) returns these same four counts, with the negative class (0) first, exactly as in Chart 1. Now that we understand the four outcomes, we can use them to calculate different evaluation metrics.

Evaluating Predictions at a Fixed Threshold

The following metrics evaluate a model's predictions at a fixed decision threshold of 0.5.

Accuracy

Accuracy measures the proportion of all predictions that are correct. In other words, it answers the question: how often did the model make the correct prediction?

Accuracy = TP + TNTP + TN + FP + FN = 9 + 820 = 0.85

Our model correctly classified 17 out of 20 customers, giving it an accuracy of 85%. In Python:

from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score

# 1 = churn, 0 = stay: first 10 customers actually churned, last 10 stayed
y_true = [1] * 10 + [0] * 10

# model predictions: 9 of the 10 churners and 8 of the 10 stayers are right
y_pred = (
    [1] * 9 + [0] * 1 +
    [1] * 2 + [0] * 8
)

accuracy = accuracy_score(y_true, y_pred)

print(accuracy)
# 0.85

Accuracy is easy to interpret and useful when classes are reasonably balanced and false positives and false negatives have similar costs. However, it can be misleading when one class is much more common than the other.

Imagine a dataset where only 5% of customers churn. A model that predicts every customer will stay would achieve 95% accuracy—without identifying a single customer who actually churns.

Despite its impressive accuracy, the model fails at its primary objective: identifying customers at risk of leaving. This is why accuracy should not be evaluated in isolation, especially when working with imbalanced datasets.

Precision

Precision measures how many predicted positive cases are actually positive. For our churn model, it answers: of all the customers predicted to churn, how many actually churned?

Precision = TPTP + FP = 99 + 2 = 911 ≈ 0.818

Our model achieves a precision of approximately 81.8%. This means that around 81.8% of the customers flagged as potential churners actually churned. In Python:

from sklearn.metrics import precision_score

precision = precision_score(y_true, y_pred)

print(precision)
# 0.8182

Precision is particularly important when false positives are costly. Imagine a company offering discounts to customers predicted to churn. If the model incorrectly flags customers who would have stayed anyway, the company may waste money on unnecessary retention campaigns. Higher precision means a greater proportion of flagged customers are actual churners, helping the company target its resources more effectively.

However, precision doesn't tell us how many actual churners the model fails to identify. To measure that, we need recall.

Recall

Recall measures the proportion of actual positive cases that the model correctly identifies. For our churn model, it answers: of all the customers who actually churned, how many did the model identify?

A high recall means the model misses fewer actual churners, making it particularly useful when failing to identify a customer at risk of leaving is costly.

Recall = TPTP + FN = 99 + 1 = 910 = 0.9

Our model achieves a recall of 90%. It successfully identified nine of the ten customers who churned. In Python:

from sklearn.metrics import recall_score

recall = recall_score(y_true, y_pred)

print(recall)
# 0.9

Recall is also known as sensitivity or the true positive rate (TPR). It is particularly important when false negatives are costly.

For example, if a company wants to identify as many potential churners as possible, missing a customer who is about to leave may be more costly than contacting someone who would have stayed anyway. In this case, prioritising recall over precision may be appropriate.

However, increasing recall often comes at a cost: the model may also flag more customers who would not have churned, reducing precision. This trade-off depends on the decision threshold and is central to choosing the right model for a business problem.

The Precision–Recall Trade-off

Precision measures how reliable positive predictions are, while recall measures how many actual positive cases the model identifies. Adjusting the decision threshold changes the balance between these two metrics.

  • Lowering the threshold. More customers are classified as potential churners. Recall generally increases because fewer actual churners are missed, but precision may decrease as more customers who would have stayed are incorrectly flagged.
  • Raising the threshold. Fewer customers are classified as potential churners. Precision may increase because positive predictions become more selective, but recall may decrease as more actual churners are missed.

The exact trade-off depends on the model's predicted probabilities and the dataset. There is no universally optimal threshold—the right choice depends on the business objective and the relative costs of false positives and false negatives.

For example, a company with limited resources for retention campaigns may prioritise precision (higher threshold) to avoid unnecessary spending. A company focused on identifying as many potential churners as possible may prioritise recall (lower threshold), even if this means contacting more customers who would have stayed.

But what if both precision and recall matter? This is where the F1 score becomes useful.

F1 Score

The F1 score combines precision and recall into a single metric using their harmonic mean.

F1 = 2 × Precision × RecallPrecision + Recall = 2 × 0.8182 × 0.90.8182 + 0.9 ≈ 0.857

Our model achieves an F1 score of approximately 85.7%. In Python:

from sklearn.metrics import f1_score

f1 = f1_score(y_true, y_pred)

print(f1)
# 0.8571

The F1 score is useful when we want to balance precision and recall, particularly when the positive class is more important than the negative class. However, it has limitations. It does not account for true negatives, and it treats precision and recall symmetrically. If false positives and false negatives have substantially different business costs, the F1 score may not reflect the objective perfectly. In such cases, it is often better to examine precision and recall separately or consider a metric that assigns different importance to them.

So far, we've evaluated the model's predictions at one particular threshold. But what if we want to understand how well the model distinguishes between churners and non-churners across different thresholds? That's where the ROC curve comes in.

Evaluating Model Performance Across Thresholds

ROC Curve and AUC

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve evaluates how well a classification model distinguishes between positive and negative cases across different decision thresholds.

It plots the true positive rate (TPR) against the false positive rate (FPR). Recall is another name for TPR:

TPR = TPTP + FN

The false positive rate measures the proportion of actual negative cases incorrectly classified as positive:

FPR = FPFP + TN

Applying the Metrics to Our Churn Model

At the original decision threshold of 0.5, our confusion matrix gives us:

TPR = 910 = 0.90    FPR = 210 = 0.20

This means the model identifies 90% of actual churners while incorrectly flagging 20% of customers who would have stayed.

How should we read the curve (Chart 3)?

  • Closer to the top-left corner. The model achieves a high true positive rate with a low false positive rate, indicating better discrimination.
  • Along the diagonal. The model performs approximately like random ranking.
  • Below the diagonal. The ranking is worse than random in the usual interpretation, although reversing the score direction can change this.

The goal is to achieve a high true positive rate while keeping the false positive rate low. The ROC curve helps us understand this trade-off across thresholds rather than evaluating the model at just one threshold.

AUC: Area Under the ROC Curve

The Area Under the ROC Curve (ROC-AUC) summarises the ROC curve in a single number, typically ranging from 0 to 1. It measures how well a model distinguishes between positive and negative cases based on their predicted scores.

How should we interpret it?

  • AUC = 0.5. The model has discrimination comparable to random ranking.
  • AUC closer to 1. The model generally ranks positive cases above negative cases more effectively.
  • AUC = 1.0. The model perfectly separates positive and negative cases by their scores.

AUC is particularly useful when comparing models because it evaluates their ability to rank cases across decision thresholds rather than focusing on a single threshold.

An AUC of 0.85 does not mean that 85% of predictions are correct. Instead, it means that, for a randomly selected positive case and a randomly selected negative case, the model assigns the positive case a higher score approximately 85% of the time, counting ties as half.

What AUC Doesn't Tell Us

A model can have a high ROC-AUC but still perform poorly at a particular decision threshold. For example, it might rank customers effectively but flag too many customers who would have stayed, resulting in unnecessary retention costs.

AUC therefore complements accuracy, precision, recall and F1 score rather than replacing them. These metrics help evaluate performance at a specific threshold, while ROC-AUC summarises discrimination across thresholds.

One further consideration is class imbalance. When positive cases are rare, ROC-AUC may not fully reflect how useful positive predictions are in practice. In such situations, the Precision–Recall (PR) curve and its associated area can provide additional insight into precision and recall for the positive class.

Calculating the ROC Curve and AUC in Python

Scikit-learn provides RocCurveDisplay to calculate and visualise the ROC curve. Importantly, the ROC curve requires probability scores or decision scores rather than only the final binary predictions.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

from sklearn.metrics import RocCurveDisplay
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

# Fit a logistic regression model
model = LogisticRegression()
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

# Obtain probabilities for the positive class
y_proba = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]

# Plot the ROC curve
RocCurveDisplay.from_predictions(y_test, y_proba)

plt.show()

Here, predict_proba() returns the estimated probability for each class. Selecting [:, 1] extracts the probability of the positive class, assumed here to represent churn. The ROC curve uses these probability scores to evaluate the model across different thresholds.

To calculate the AUC from the same probabilities, we can use roc_auc_score:

from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, y_proba)

print(auc)

The result is a single value summarising the model's ability to distinguish between positive and negative cases.

Which Metric Should You Use?

We've explored several evaluation metrics, but which one should you prioritise? It depends on what the model is designed to achieve and which mistakes matter most.

  • Accuracy. Use it when classes are reasonably balanced and false positives and false negatives have similar costs. It measures the proportion of correct predictions.
  • Precision. Prioritise it when false positives are costly. It tells you how many positive predictions are actually positive.
  • Recall. Prioritise it when false negatives are costly. It measures how many actual positive cases the model correctly identifies.
  • F1 score. Use it when both precision and recall matter. It combines the two into a single score using their harmonic mean.
  • ROC-AUC. Use it to compare models' ability to distinguish between positive and negative cases across decision thresholds.
  • Precision–Recall curve and PR-AUC. Consider these when positive cases are rare. They provide insight into the trade-off between precision and recall across thresholds.

Putting It into Practice

Let's revisit our customer churn example and consider a few other applications.

  • Customer churn prediction. If the goal is to identify as many potential churners as possible, recall may be the priority. If retention campaigns are expensive, precision becomes more important because the company wants to avoid targeting customers who would stay anyway.
  • Fraud detection. Missing fraudulent transactions can be costly, making recall important. However, too many false alarms can disrupt legitimate transactions, so precision matters too.
  • Medical screening. Missing a person with a serious condition can have significant consequences. Recall may therefore be prioritised, while precision remains important for limiting unnecessary follow-up tests.
  • Comparing classification models. ROC-AUC helps compare models' ability to rank positive cases above negative cases across thresholds. When positive cases are rare, PR-AUC can provide additional insight into how well the models identify the positive class.

Metric selection should follow the problem, not the other way around. Before choosing a metric, ask yourself:

  • Which type of error is more costly?
  • Are the classes balanced or imbalanced?
  • Do I need reliable positive predictions, high coverage of actual positive cases, or both?
  • Am I evaluating predictions at a specific threshold or comparing models across thresholds?

These questions help translate model evaluation into meaningful business decisions. The best metric is not necessarily the one with the highest score, but the one that best reflects the problem you are trying to solve.

Key takeaways

  • The confusion matrix is the foundation of classification evaluation. It shows the four possible outcomes: true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives.
  • Accuracy measures overall correctness, precision tells us how reliable positive predictions are, and recall measures how many actual positive cases the model identifies. The F1 score balances precision and recall.
  • The ROC curve and AUC evaluate the model's ability to distinguish between positive and negative cases across decision thresholds. An AUC of 0.85 does not mean that 85% of predictions are correct.
  • No single metric tells the whole story. The right choice depends on the business objective and the costs of different mistakes. A good model is not simply one with a high score, but one whose performance meets the needs of the problem it was built to solve.
